# Instruments and controls: interaction patterns transferable to a one-screen phone retirement instrument

Scope: physical and digital instruments whose gesture, encoding and live feedback could be transferred to Someday's home screen (six inputs as one object; £/month after tax, years it lasts, confidence across 2,000 futures, all moving while the finger is on the control). Research date 31 October 2026. Where a source could not be read in full (scanned PDF, 403, JS-only page) it is flagged. UK English throughout.

---

## Key question 1 — Aircraft PFDs/HUDs and car instrument clusters: how do they show several live values and a trend in one glance, and what visual grammar is transferable?

### Takeaway
Avionics solved "many live values, one glance" with a fixed grammar: a central reference (attitude/flight-path marker), moving tapes on the flanks with a fixed pointer, and a short **trend vector** that projects the value a few seconds ahead. Car clusters (Taycan, Polestar, Lucid, Rivian) converge on three things — a strict centre/flank layout, a "calm" mode that empties the centre, and 3D used only where it carries meaning. The transferable grammar for Someday is: fixed pointer + moving scale (not moving pointer), a trend/projection cue drawn as a thin coloured line extending from the current value, colour-banded ranges on the scale itself rather than separate labels, and an empty centre reserved for the single headline number.

### Cited Findings
- **Basic-T and the two flanking strips.** A 1987 NASA simulation study notes the available display space for air-data instruments is restricted to two strips either side of the attitude director indicator, which is why vertical tapes were adopted; the "basic T" puts airspeed and altitude either side of attitude with heading below — [NASA NTRS 19870010832](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19870010832).
- **Tape = moving scale, fixed pointer.** The same report: vertical tape formats "display information by moving the 'dial', i.e., the scale or tape, and leaving the indicator, i.e., pointer, fixed." Round dials are better for judging rate of change; the tape gives a compact macro-view — [NASA NTRS 19870010832](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19870010832).
- **Orientation convention and workload.** The NASA study found pilots preferred larger numbers at the top of the tape, reporting lower workload; formats *without* a trend indicator produced better secondary-task performance, but no difference in airspeed or altitude tracking — [NASA NTRS 19870010832](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19870010832).
- **Tapes blur at high rates of change.** A Dynamic Non-Linear Display (DNLD) proposal argues "the tape altimeter becomes a useless blur during high-rate maneuvers" and splits the tape into a high-resolution central linear region with compressed outer regions — [Tech Briefs contest entry (unverified, contest listing)](https://contest.techbriefs.com/2026/entries/aerospace-and-defense/14469-0708-151940-dynamic-non-linear-display-dnld-flight-instruments). A Honeywell patent describes a non-linear altitude tape showing ±1,200 ft at any time — [US 7205907](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7205907).
- **Garmin G1000 trend vector.** The airspeed trend vector is a vertical magenta line extending from the current airspeed pointer; "the end of the trend vector displays approximately what the airspeed will be in 6 seconds if the current rate of acceleration/deceleration is maintained"; it disappears when airspeed is steady and, if it crosses Vne, the readout changes colour and an aural "OVERSPEED" alert fires — [Garmin G1000 webhelp: Airspeed Indicator](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-7317DC85-4516-4684-BBAF-FD7BE84D5E86/EN-US/GUID-1AA187F5-8E8A-480C-9F0F-D094D005E144.html); [Garmin G1000 manual p.35](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/2069144/Garmin-G1000.html?page=35). The six-second figure may vary by unit/software — [MSFS forum discussion](https://forums.flightsimulator.com/t/reading-air-speed-on-the-garmin-g-1000/321311). The Avidyne PFD shows its trend only above ~0.8 kt/s — [Avidyne PFD guide (BEFA)](https://befa.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Avionics-Avidyne-PFD.pdf).
- **HUD grammar: centre reference, scales wrapped round it.** In the F-16 HUD (FlightGear documentation of the real symbology) the flight-path marker and pitch ladder sit at centre; airspeed scale left, altitude scale right, heading tape top; digital readouts in corners; the FPM's offset from the bore-cross read against the ladder *is* angle of attack — [F-16 HUD wiki](https://github.com/NikolaiVChr/f16/wiki/HUD).
- **Energy/acceleration cue.** A NASA V/STOL HUD report names an "Acceleration Caret" whose position relative to the flight-path symbol encodes longitudinal acceleration — [NASA NTRS 19900008316](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19900008316/downloads/19900008316.pdf). Honeywell's HGS guide lists "inertial flight path and flight path acceleration" symbology as supporting energy management — [HGS Pilot Guide (Scribd copy)](https://www.scribd.com/document/490055220/HGS-Pilot-Guide-5600-pdf). The term "energy caret" itself was not found defined in an authoritative source (gap).
- **Boeing rollout deceleration symbology.** A scale of marks with an acceleration symbol below the boresight shows actual deceleration against required — [US 6107943](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6107943).
- **Porsche Taycan "tubes".** Porsche's own help page: fully digital cluster in a three-tube layout, each tube configurable; left tube = driver assistance/G-force/tyre pressure/charging; centre tube = power meter, 3D assistance view, map (standard/expanded/minimal); right tube = Sport Chrono/trip/nav/media; tubes are changed with a toggle switch and scroll wheel on the steering wheel and saved to the driver profile — [Porsche: Instrument cluster Taycan](https://ask.porsche.com/gb/en-GB/instrument-cluster-taycan/). A week-long UX review called it "excessive configurability … numerous cluster layouts and widgets" — [The Turn Signal: Taycan UX review](https://theturnsignal.pages.dev/driving-a-porsche-taycan-for-a-week-a-ux-review); an owner found the density "rather sparse … tyre pressure takes up an entire screen" — [Taycan forum thread](https://taycanforum.com/forum/threads/taycan-instrument-cluster-tubes-information-density.7425/latest).
- **Polestar 2 "Calm" mode.** The owner's manual: "The driver display has two different display modes that are changed via the button on the right-hand side of the steering wheel. The middle section of the driver display is empty in calm mode." Later manuals list calm / surround / map, and a "Minimalistic" theme — [Polestar 2 manual 2023](https://www.polestar.com/uk/manual/polestar-2/2023/article/36c64f5c6770d5acc0a8015145cdb2ce/); [Polestar 3 manual](https://www.polestar.com/en-sg/manual/polestar-3/2027/article/47d2c97fd33effd3c0a8cc3718c999b7-ee82b598d3b6d663c0a8cc3706944a9f-8664b2fa77a7e089c0a8296870d1a409/). An owner-blogger: beyond the map and cruise set-speed "that's pretty much it" — [Klement on Investing](https://klementoninvesting.substack.com/p/the-beauty-of-simplicity).
- **Lucid Air.** A comparison piece describes Lucid's UI as "minimalist and dark, monochrome", with 3D limited to "where it brings value" (e.g. the speedometer) and animated feedback such as charging used to communicate status — [engineered.at: Mercedes vs Lucid](https://engineered.at/articles/mercedes-vs-lucid-different-views-on-luxury-ui-design-f3e8da54-112e-4c3d-9bab-b8f26e3bf1c2). Motor1 (via inkl) called it "reinvention done right" — [inkl/Motor1 review](https://inkl.com/news/lucid-s-infotainment-system-is-reinvention-done-right-review).
- **Rivian.** Carsguide describes the R1 cluster as "a three-dial digital version of the brand's five-dial instrument cluster, with about 5,000 possible configurations" — [Carsguide R1S vs Taycan](https://www.carsguide.com.au/rivian/r1s/vs/porsche-taycan). The 2024 redesign moved to illustrated, game-like visuals and extended Unreal Engine from the driver display to the whole infotainment screen — [InsideEVs 2024 review](https://insideevs.com/reviews/735802/rivian-r1s-infotainment-review-2024/); [Unreal Engine spotlight (403 on fetch; title only)](https://www.unrealengine.com/spotlights/rivian-brings-adventurous-spirit-to-new-display-ui-powered-by-unreal-engine); [Fast Company (403 on fetch)](https://www.fastcompany.com/91135876/3-details-from-rivians-r1-redesign). Owners ask for "range at arrival" in the cluster and complain the clock is hard to read at speed — [Rivian forums thread](https://rivianforums.com/forum/threads/driver-display-needs-more-customization.47730/post-829053).
- **Mercedes MBUX Hyperscreen "zero layer".** Mercedes: "To get to the most important applications, the user must scroll through 0 menu levels. That's why Mercedes-Benz calls this zero layer"; suggestion "magic modules" are accepted or rejected with one click — [Mercedes-Benz Group: MBUX Hyperscreen](https://group.mercedes-benz.com/innovation/digitalisierung/konnektivitaet/mbux-hyperscreen.html); [Mercedes-Benz Canada press release](https://media.mercedes-benz.ca/releases/mercedes-benz-unveils-the-new-mbux-hyperscreen-which-will-debut-in-the-eqs).

### Inferences
- The avionics convention (fixed pointer, moving scale) maps directly onto a drum/dial input: the finger drags the *scale*, the read-off position stays put, and the headline number stays in the same pixel location — which is also what the brief wants ("the answer should move while the hand is still on the control").
- A six-second trend vector is a ready-made encoding for Someday's mid-gesture feedback: as the stop-age or spend scale is dragged, a short line from the current £/month marker shows where the answer is heading, in the accent colour, vanishing when the hand stops. It also answers the brief's "honest" criterion if the line's length is driven by the model's sensitivity, not by animation easing.
- The NASA finding that trend cues *didn't* improve tracking and cost secondary-task performance is a caution: keep exactly one trend cue on screen, never one per input.
- Colour-coded speed-range strips on the airspeed tape (white/green/yellow/red arcs painted *on the scale*) are the model for showing "which stop-ages hold" directly on the stop-age drum, removing the need for a separate "edge" map on the home screen.
- Polestar's empty centre and Lucid's "3D only where it carries meaning" are the clearest production examples of the brief's §3 minimalism rules; the Taycan's over-configurable tubes are the counter-example (nothing should be configurable on the home).
- The MBUX "zero layer" is the product-language equivalent of the brief's "one screen, no depth by scrolling": everything that matters is at level zero and anything deeper is an invitation, not a menu.

### Gaps
- No authoritative definition of "energy caret" HUD symbology was found; only "acceleration caret/cue" in NASA and Honeywell material.
- No detailed design write-up of the Lucid Glass Cockpit's own cluster (speed/range/energy rendering) was found; only secondary commentary.
- The Rivian Unreal Engine spotlight and Fast Company piece returned 403; their specifics (frame rate, animation principles) are unverified.
- Mercedes "Hyperscreen" cluster-side visual grammar (as opposed to the zero-layer concept) was not described in any source read.

## Key question 2 — Watch crowns and complications (watchOS, Apple Watch Ultra, Garmin): how does one rotary control set many values with haptics, and how do radial complications pack information?

### Takeaway
Apple's model is one rotary input whose *meaning is whatever has focus*: the Crown scrolls, pages, or edits a value depending on the focused element, with linear haptic detents by default and a hard rule that every turn must produce visible change at the speed of the turn. Complications pack a value, its range and its trend into open/closed/segmented gauges with 2 pt minimum strokes and SF Rounded numerals, with the gauge style chosen by the *kind* of number (percentage of a whole vs arbitrary range vs fast-changing). Both rules transfer cleanly: one drag gesture, focus decides which of the six inputs it edits, and the answer is a gauge whose style matches its semantics.

### Cited Findings
- **Crown as primary input; meaning follows focus.** Apple HIG: "Starting with watchOS 10, the Digital Crown takes on an elevated role as the primary input for navigation … Within apps, people turn the Digital Crown to switch between vertically paginated tabs, and to scroll through list views." Apps don't receive Crown presses (reserved by the system) — [Apple HIG: Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown).
- **Default haptics.** "By default, the system provides linear haptic detents — or taps — as people turn the Digital Crown a specific distance. Some system controls, like table views, provide detents as new items scroll onto the screen." Turn detents off "if the default detents don't match your app's animation" — [Apple HIG: Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown).
- **Visible feedback is mandatory; speed must track hand speed.** "If you don't provide visual feedback, people are likely to assume that turning the Digital Crown has no effect in your app." "Update your interface to match the speed with which people turn the Digital Crown … Avoid updating content at a rate that makes it difficult for people to select values." — [Apple HIG: Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown).
- **Crown as data inspector (the World Clock precedent).** "In contexts where the Digital Crown doesn't need to navigate … it's a great tool to inspect data in your app. For example, in World Clock, turning the Digital Crown advances the time of day at a selected location, allowing people to compare various times of day" — [Apple HIG: Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown).
- **Back every Crown interaction with touch.** "When anchoring interactions to the Digital Crown, also be sure to back them up with corresponding touch screen interactions." — [Apple HIG: Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown).
- **Wheel pickers on watchOS.** "Pickers display lists of items that people navigate using the Digital Crown, which helps people manage selections in a precise and engaging way." The navigation-link style lets a person "scrub through the options using the Digital Crown without tapping the button" — [Apple HIG: Pickers](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/pickers).
- **Gauge styles chosen by the semantics of the number.** "The closed style can convey a value that's a percentage of a whole, such as for a battery gauge. The open style works well when the minimum and maximum values are arbitrary … like for a speed indicator. … the segmented style also displays values within an app-defined range, and can convey rapid value changes, such as in the Noise complication." — [Apple HIG: Complications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/complications).
- **Open-gauge range text.** A circular open gauge runs from the 8 o'clock to the 4 o'clock position (≈240°) with a central number and two small range numbers at 6 o'clock (e.g. "55" green left, "76" orange right) — described in the HIG image captions — [Apple HIG: Complications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/complications).
- **Bezel text.** Curved text along the bezel "can fill nearly 180 degrees of the bezel before truncating" — [Apple HIG: Complications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/complications).
- **Legibility floors.** "When creating complication content, generally use line widths of two points or greater. Thinner lines can be difficult to see at a glance, especially when the wearer is in motion." Default text: Rounded, Medium, 12–14.5 pt in circular; 34.5–41 pt in extra-large circular; 16.5–19.5 pt rectangular — [Apple HIG: Complications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/complications).
- **Tinted mode: don't rely on colour.** "Avoid using color as the only way to communicate important information. You want people to get the same information in tinted mode as they do in nontinted mode." — [Apple HIG: Complications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/complications).
- **Rectangular = trend over time.** "The Heart Rate complication displays a graph of heart-rate values within a 24-hour period. The graph uses high-contrast white and red for the primary content and a lower-contrast gray for the graph lines and labels" — [Apple HIG: Complications](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/complications).
- **Activity rings — three nested rings, fixed semantics.** Apple tested "literally hundreds of different concepts to communicate this 3-dimensional way of looking at your all-day activity" (Blahnik memo) — [9to5Mac: Jay Blahnik memo](https://9to5mac.com/2015/03/09/jay-blahnik-memo/); the rings were chosen as "simple, elegant, unique, easy to view, visually motivating and fun" — [Engadget 2015](https://www.engadget.com/2015-03-10-apple-watch-activity-design.html). HIG rules: never change colours, always on black, keep a margin ≥ ring spacing, "Don't use Activity rings for decoration", specified RGB (Move 250,17,79; Exercise 166,255,0; Stand 0,255,246) — [Apple HIG: Activity rings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/activity-rings).
- **Garmin Body Battery.** A 0–100 "fuel gauge" with four bands; the glance shows the current level plus a graph of recent hours; blue = rest, orange = stress, grey = too active to read — [Garmin Forerunner 255 manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/forerunner255series/EN-SG/GUID-708836C9-AAAC-4D9B-9A71-73361F888AB0-627.html); [Garmin Forerunner 165 manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/forerunner165series/EN-SG/GUID-4CC69588-C506-4A49-8F34-662B56ADD18A-6341.html).
- **Carrot Weather on watchOS.** Four customisable data slots with a live preview "that reflects each change"; editing "resembles editing a watch face" — [MacStories review](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/carrot-weather-adds-powerful-redesigned-apple-watch-app/).

### Inferences
- On a phone there is no crown, but a single on-screen drum/ring that behaves like one (focus decides which input it edits; the drum's own tick marks are the detents; the browser's `navigator.vibrate` is unavailable on iOS Safari, so "haptics" must be visual/audio) reproduces the watchOS model. Apple's rule that update speed must track hand speed argues for computing the deterministic answer synchronously per pointer-move and letting the Monte Carlo confidence lag by one frame — the engine already runs MC in a worker.
- The World Clock precedent ("turn to compare") is the exact gesture Someday needs for the plan-to age and stop age: turning does not "set a field", it *inspects* the plan at a different age and the answer updates.
- Someday's confidence (share of 2,000 futures) is a percentage of a whole → *closed* gauge. "How long it lasts" has an arbitrary range (now → plan-to age) → *open* gauge with range text at the bottom (today's age left, plan-to age right). The HIG's semantic gauge selection removes a design decision.
- Nested rings only work when the rings have *fixed, never-reassigned* meanings (Apple forbids re-use); if Someday uses rings for six inputs, each ring must keep one meaning forever and be labelled by position, not colour alone (tinted-mode rule).
- 2 pt minimum strokes at watch size imply ≥ 3 px at phone size for any gauge or trend line, and large numerals (34–41 pt on a 40–49 mm face) scale to roughly 56–72 px for the headline £/month on a 390 px screen.

### Gaps
- Apple Watch Ultra–specific crown/Action-button guidance was not separately read (the HIG Digital Crown page covers all models; Ultra differences appear only as 49 mm size rows).
- No Garmin design rationale document for arc gauges was found; only manual descriptions.
- No Oura design review from a credible outlet was found; the 2025 redesign is reported only by 9to5Google (not fetched) and low-quality blogs — [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/10/20/oura-app-redesign/).

## Key question 3 — Hardware UIs (Teenage Engineering OP-1/OP-Z/TP-7, Elektron, Nothing, Braun/Rams, camera mode dials, Nest ring, iPod click wheel): what makes a dial feel like one object with many settings, and how do they indicate position, range and active parameter?

### Takeaway
The successful hardware instruments share four mechanics: (1) **colour or position binds a control to its on-screen parameter** (OP-1's four coloured encoders; Rams's red FM key matching red FM scale lettering); (2) **the display shows the value's *consequence*, not just the number** (Nest shows time-to-temperature; OP-1 animates the sound); (3) **detents/clicks are tied one-to-one to scale marks, with pitch encoding direction** (Nest); (4) **settings are legible with the device off** (Fujifilm dials). The failures are instructive too: Elektron's shared encoders force users to work out "which knob relates to which parameter", and the touch-screen rotary in Mixxx is "almost useless" while sliders work — rotation on glass needs a large radius and must be bound to a visible scale.

### Cited Findings
- **OP-1: colour binds knob to graphic.** Teenage Engineering: "the four color encoders are related to the graphical interface on the display"; "A green graphical element or text hints that the green encoder will change its value or position" — [TE OP-1 layout guide](https://teenage.engineering/guides/op-1/original/layout); [TE OP-1 overview](https://teenage.engineering/products/op-1/original/overview). The screen is a 320×160 AMOLED at 60 fps; the knobs change function by mode — [MusicRadar review](https://www.musicradar.com/reviews/tech/teenage-engineering-op-1-504300); [Sonic State review](https://sonicstate.com/news/2011/08/18/sonic-lab-teenage-engineering-op-1-review/).
- **TP-7: a motorised wheel that does real work.** The centre reel scrubs audio and scrolls menus "a bit like the mechanical wheel on the original iPod. There's even a bump of haptic feedback when you spin it" — [Stuff](https://www.stuff.tv/news/teenage-engineering-tp-7-is-the-most-desirable-dictaphone-ever-made/); brushed motor plus hall sensor for "tactile manipulation of the audio" — [Signal Sounds listing](https://www.signalsounds.com/teenage-engineering-tp-7-field-recorder); Domus calls it "over-engineered, mechanically complicated tactile candy in the form of a spinning (fake) tape reel" — [Domus](https://domusweb.it/en/news/gallery/2023/05/19/teenage-engineerings-new-recorder-is-a-triumph-of-physical-skeuomorphism.html).
- **Elektron: shared encoders, page-based.** Sound On Sound: endless encoders with "noticeably higher resolution" working alongside the screen; parameter locks by holding a step — [Sound On Sound Digitakt review](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/elektron-digitakt). Users: "I often find myself having to work out which knob relates to which parameter, especially on screens that don't have eight parameters" — [Elektronauts thread](https://www.elektronauts.com/t/digitakt-interface-design/41068).
- **Nest: one ring, rotate + press, click per degree.** Cooper Hewitt: turning the outer ring raises or lowers the temperature; pushing the ring opens a menu; the rotating interface "recalls the classic design of Henry Dreyfuss's Honeywell Round"; drawings by Eric Daniels and John Benjamin Filson — [Cooper Hewitt: Drawings for Nest](https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2014/11/12/drawings-for-nest-thermostat/). Nest's design team describe "a subtle, yet satisfying and surprisingly rich 'click' sound" with "each click matching a temperature degree hashmark on screen" and pitch rising/falling with direction — [Nest design blog on Medium (403 on fetch; quoted from search snippet)](https://medium.com/building-for-the-home/designing-a-new-member-of-the-thermostat-family-36c81ebc5cb9). Patents: navigation "by virtue of respective rotational input motions and inward pressings" — [US 2013/0046397](https://www.patenthub.cn/cpc/patent-182196-US2013046397A1-8604903a94fa42069ce48193e7f73dcf.html); [US 2012/0131504](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20120131504).
- **iPod click wheel.** Capacitive ring ("conductive membrane beneath the plastic covering"), four mechanical buttons under the ring plus select button; first on iPod mini, last on iPod nano 5G; "a faster-moving finger produces a more concentrated stream of signals" — [Wikipedia: iPod click wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPod_click_wheel). Phil Schiller presented the scroll-wheel mock-ups and suggested menus should scroll faster the longer the wheel is turned — [Wired: The Perfect Thing](https://wirednews.pages.dev/story/the-perfect-thing); [MacRumors: story of the iPod](https://macrumors.com/2006/10/17/the-story-of-the-ipod). Fadell's "iPod phone" prototype had a click wheel that turned into a number pad and "turned out not to be practical" — [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2022/05/09/this-weird-iphone-prototype-has-an-ipod-click-wheel-that-turns-into-a-number-pad/).
- **Fujifilm dials: legible when off.** "Dials are not about being nostalgic of the old days, but rather they are in place for their practicality"; the setting can be read "at a glance" and "without turning the camera on"; the X-Pro2 shutter/ISO dial has 22 positions, 38 parts, electronically linked — [Fujifilm X Stories: Dials, Inside Out](https://www.fujifilm-x.com/global/stories/dials-inside-out/). Fstoppers: dedicated dials are "a choice that prioritizes understanding over convenience" — [Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/originals/why-fujifilm-understands-its-customers-better-any-other-camera-company-902330). Removing dials on the X-S10 drew criticism that "now you must go into the menus to change basic settings" — [jmpeltier](https://www.jmpeltier.com/fujifilm-killed-off-custom-settings/).
- **Rams: colour binds control to scale.** Rams on the T 1000: it mattered that products were "as self-explanatory as possible", citing "the red symbol on the tuner switch, the red FM key and the matching red scale lettering for FM" — [Design und Text interview with Rams](https://www.designundtext.com/en/2.1.10_braun-design-interview-rams-pbdd.php); T 1000 object record — [Brooklyn Museum](https://brooklynmuseum.org/opencollection/objects/170035).
- **Touchscreen rotary is hard; sliders are fine.** Mixxx bug report: "controlling a Mixxx rotary dial via currently tested touchscreens is really bad to the point of being almost useless (interestingly, slider controls work fine)" — [Launchpad bug 1217738](https://bugs.launchpad.net/bugs/1217738) (single user report).
- **Tangible knobs 20% faster than virtual.** Knobology Revisited (ITS '15, 20 participants): tangible knobs ~20% faster than virtual touch knobs and kept performance outside the locus of attention (peripheral/eyes-free) — [RWTH Aachen: Knobology Revisited](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/knobology).
- **In-car: rotary wins for continuous adjustment, touch for alphanumerics.** Rydström et al. 2009: "the touch screen interface was better for alphanumeric input tasks and the interface manoeuvred by a rotary control was better for continuous adjustments and list scrolling" — [SAFER research library](https://www.saferresearch.com/library/comparison-two-contemporary-types-car-multifunctional-interfaces); SAE 2017: with haptic feedback the rotary controller kept drivers' eyes on the road better for simple continuous control — [SAE 2017-01-1386](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/still-look-up-remote-rotary-controller-vs-touchscreen-2017-01-1386).

### Inferences
- The OP-1/Rams binding rule gives Someday its label-free grammar: the active segment of the instrument and the number it drives share the single accent colour; everything else is monochrome. That satisfies "if a label can be replaced by position or motion, it should be."
- Nest's one-click-per-hashmark with pitch for direction is reproducible in the browser as a visual tick-snap plus an optional short Web Audio click whose pitch rises when a value improves the plan and falls when it worsens it — a non-visual channel for the answer's direction that also serves screen-reader users if announced.
- Fujifilm's "legible when off" means an untouched Someday instrument must still read as a complete statement of the six inputs — positions on the dial *are* the summary, so no separate recap row.
- The Elektron and Mixxx findings set two hard constraints for an on-glass dial: never share one dial across parameters without a persistent, colour-bound indication of which is active; and give rotation a large radius (a ring hugging the 390 px width, not a 60 px knob), or convert rotation to a linear drag along an arc so that accuracy approaches slider accuracy.
- The failed iPod-phone (wheel that becomes a keypad) warns against drums that change *kind* of control mid-use; a Someday drum should always be a drum.

- No controlled study of touchscreen rotary vs touchscreen slider accuracy was found (only the Mixxx report and the tangible-vs-virtual knob study).

## Key question 4 — Radial and rotary menus (pie/marking menus; Blender, Maya; Apple Pencil Pro squeeze; Figma) and the HCI evidence on radial vs linear, dials vs sliders, angular Fitts's law and crossing

### Takeaway
The evidence base is strong and consistent: radial selection is faster than linear (≈15% in Callahan et al. 1988) with fewer errors because distance is fixed and target width grows with radius; marks are ~3.5× faster than menu pop-ups once learned; 8 items per level is the reliable ceiling (12 only when items sit on compass axes); half-pie menus work for one-handed thumbs after ~5 minutes' practice. Fitts's law extends to rotation by substituting angular amplitude and width (Stoelen & Akin 2010), and tactile controls beat touch for accuracy. Crossing (passing through a goal rather than landing on it) is a Fitts-governed, pointing-equivalent primitive that suits continuous drags. For Someday: six segments is comfortably inside the 8-item limit; put the two most-used inputs (stop age, monthly take-home) on the vertical/horizontal axes; and make the "set" gesture a continuous crossing/drag, not a tap.

### Cited Findings
- **Pie vs linear, 1988.** Callahan, Hopkins, Weiser & Shneiderman, CHI '88: pie menus offer "reduced target seek time, lower error rates … by fixing the distance factor and increasing the target size in Fitts's Law", and are "generally subjectively equivalent" — [UMD abstract](https://sites.umiacs.umd.edu/node/16962). Secondary accounts give ~15% faster with fewer errors across all task groupings; 15 menus × 4 repetitions per user; disadvantage: pie menus take more screen area — [Hopkins, 30-year retrospective (OSnews)](https://www.osnews.com/story/30371/pie-menus-a-30-year-retrospective/); [DSHR blog](https://blog.dshr.org/2018/05/pie-menus.html). The 15% figure is from co-author write-ups, not the paper text (flag).
- **Marking menus — limits (Kurtenbach & Buxton, CHI '93).** 12 right-handed subjects, 13 menu configurations, 288 trials per data point. Error rates stayed below 10% up to breadth 8 at depth 2; 4-item menus stayed under 10% even four levels deep; beyond breadth 8/depth 2 "selection became error-prone, even for experts". Pen 1.69 s vs mouse 2.07 s. Breadth-12 menus: on-axis items (12-3-6-9 o'clock) strongly faster and more accurate than off-axis (F(2,22)=104.84 for time; 36.2 for errors). Guideline: "Place frequently used items on-axis (up, down, left, right)". Marks were ~3.5× faster than popping the menu (0.2 s vs 0.7 s for one user); with practice marks used >90% of the time; six-item menus in a real app were "perceived as error-free by experts" — [Bill Buxton: MMExpert.html (full text)](https://www.billbuxton.com/MMExpert.html); PDF — [Autodesk Research](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/the-limits-of-expert.pdf_recyw5wL6ekPqcE4Q.pdf).
- **Eyes-free property.** Marking menus "can be operated 'eyes free' because selection is based on direction of movement, not position"; an even number of items works better; the field study covered one application and two users (preliminary) — [Berkeley summary of Kurtenbach & Buxton CHI '94](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~fox/summaries/ui/marking_menus.html); paper — [Autodesk Research](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/user-learning-and-performance.pdf_recFBbPwmG7SW9uDe.pdf). Kurtenbach's thesis abstract: 4, 8 and 12 item menus advantageous for marks — [Autodesk Research thesis](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/the-design-and-evaluation.pdf_recCzWoTgz02K2Ubb.pdf).
- **Half-pie menus on a 5-inch phone, one-handed.** Yang, Chen & Hung 2017 (BIT): 36 participants walking + 8 standing; half-pie menu efficiency "improved with about five minutes of practice and then closely matched" the right-side linear menu; shorter thumbs more efficient; 137 participants rated an improved version "Good" on SUS — [Behaviour & Information Technology 36(9) abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/tbitxx/v36y2017i9p935-954.html). Related thumb-reach semicircular designs ArchMenu/ThumbMenu — [RWTH Aachen (Nowak 2019)](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/publications/nowak2019a.pdf); dwell time is the dominant factor in swipe-based pie navigation efficiency — [IJOE 2020](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-joe/article/view/17763).
- **Blender pie menus — the shipped mechanics.** "Press the key(s), move the mouse slightly toward an item, and release. The item nearest the mouse direction activates immediately." Release without moving keeps the menu open for a click. "An open disc at the center shows the current direction and highlights the selected item. A direction counts only when the pointer touches or extends beyond that disc." — [Blender Manual: Menus](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/interface/controls/buttons/menus.html).
- **Angular Fitts's law.** Stoelen & Akin (2010) extended Fitts's law to rotation by replacing linear width and amplitude with angular width ω and amplitude α, with good fit for smooth continuous rotation; there is "no literature regarding its effectiveness in terms of Fitts' law" for *detented* knobs — reported in [van Zon et al., Human Factors 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7416334).
- **Tactile vs touch accuracy (flight deck).** van Zon et al. 2019, 14 participants: heading knob 96% accuracy / 1.80 bits/s; CDU keypad 99% / 5.20 bits/s; touch navigation display 95% / 3.88 bits/s; the authors attribute the CDU's lead to "the tactile and physical nature of the interface"; a left-handed participant scored 70% on touch vs 96–99% on physical controls — [PMC7416334](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7416334).
- **Crossing interfaces.** Accot & Zhai, CHI 2002: goal crossing follows a Fitts-type law and "can be as efficient as pointing-and-clicking"; where targets can be cascaded crossing "might be faster than point-and-click"; crossing is as expressive as point-and-click — [IBM Research listing](https://research.ibm.com/publications/more-than-dotting-the-is-foundations-for-crossing-based-interfaces); [Wikipedia: Crossing-based interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing-based_interface); CrossY — [Apitz & Guimbretière, UIST '04](https://www.lri.fr/~mbl/FundHCI/papers/Apitz-UIST04.pdf); parameters for crossing dialog boxes — [Guimbretière et al.](https://researchgate.net/profile/Francois-Guimbretiere/publication/221515451_Optimal_Parameters_for_Efficient_Crossing-Based_Dialog_Boxes/links/551f9dbe0cf2a2d9e14075eb/Optimal-Parameters-for-Efficient-Crossing-Based-Dialog-Boxes.pdf). The original PDF was fetched but its text stream could not be extracted, so the per-condition index-of-performance values are not reported here (gap).
- **Apple Pencil Pro squeeze palette.** Squeeze shows a tool palette whose "contents change depending on the app" — [iPhoneJD review](https://www.iphonejd.com/?p=92); Pigment 3.5 ships a radial tool palette on squeeze (colour palette, shader, undo/redo) — [Pixite: Pigment 3.5](https://support.pixiteapps.com/hc/en-us/articles/42400765431187-Pigment-3-5); users ask for reorderable radial slots — [Goodnotes feedback](https://feedback.goodnotes.com/forums/191274-customer-suggestions-for-goodnotes/suggestions/48436646-customize-palette); [Shapr3D forum](https://discourse.shapr3d.com/t/apple-pencil-pro-squeeze-feature-for-customizable-radial-menu/33183).
- **Patents on hybrid radial/linear menus** (Kurtenbach/Autodesk) — [US 5689667](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5689667); [US 5926178](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5926178).

### Inferences
- Six inputs → six segments is inside the 8-item ceiling with margin; the two "someday" inputs (stop age, take-home) belong at 12 and 6 o'clock (on-axis), who/ages and later-life at 3 and 9, pensions and savings/guaranteed income in the diagonals — the on-axis advantage is large (F≈105) and free.
- Blender's "direction counts only beyond the central disc" is a ready dead-zone rule for a touch dial: ignore movement within ~22 px of the centre so a tap on the headline does not change an input.
- Angular Fitts says accuracy depends on angular width: a 240° open arc divided into the stop-age range 50–75 gives ~9.6° per year on a 300 px-diameter ring (~25 px of arc per year) — above a comfortable touch resolution, which supports one-year detents without a separate fine control; monthly take-home at £50 steps across £1,000–£6,000 (100 steps) would be ~2.4°/step, too fine for direct angle, so rotation there must be *velocity-scaled* (iPod acceleration) or the arc must wrap more than once (drum).
- Crossing suits the "set and see" requirement: the value is committed continuously as the finger *passes* ticks, never on release, so the answer updates mid-gesture by construction.
- Marking-menu "marks" (direction without looking) suggest a learnable shortcut: a flick from the centre toward a segment focuses that input without a menu, which an expert will do eyes-free.

### Gaps
- Exact per-condition index-of-performance values from Accot & Zhai 2002 could not be extracted (PDF text stream compressed; no pdftotext on this machine).
- No study directly comparing on-glass rotary input with on-glass sliders under Fitts's law was found; the closest are the tangible-vs-virtual knob study and the Mixxx report.
- Maya marking-menu documentation and Figma's own radial/rotary interactions were not verified from primary sources in this pass.
- The Callahan 1988 "15% faster" figure comes from co-author write-ups, not the paper text.

## Key question 5 — Products that use a single physical-feeling dial or drum on a phone touchscreen (iOS date drums, alarm/sleep dials, Material clock dial, Things/Fantastical, Carrot, Citymapper, Nike/Strava rings, Fitness rings, Oura): how do they handle many parameters without a form?

### Takeaway
Shipped phone UIs that avoid forms do it with one of three devices: a **drum** (iOS wheel pickers — several adjacent drums, one swipe each, value read at a fixed centre line), a **two-handled ring** (Apple Health/Clock sleep schedule — two handles on one dial, the arc between them *is* the derived value and it turns orange the moment the goal is missed), or a **clock dial with sequential focus** (Material 3 time picker — the same dial sets hour then minute). None of them handle six independent parameters on one dial; they either stack drums side by side or change what the dial means over time. The transferable move for Someday is the sleep-schedule pattern generalised: one ring, the handles are the inputs, the arc/fill between them is the answer, and colour flips when the plan stops holding.

### Cited Findings
- **iOS wheel pickers.** Apple HIG: "A picker displays one or more scrollable lists of distinct values"; wheels style = "a set of scrolling wheels that also supports data entry through built-in or external keyboards"; "Consider using a picker to offer medium-to-long lists of items … it may add too much visual weight to a short list"; "Use predictable and logically ordered values. Before people interact with a picker, many of its values can be hidden"; "Avoid switching views to show a picker"; minute granularity can be coarsened (0/15/30/45) — [Apple HIG: Pickers](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/pickers).
- **Apple Health / Clock sleep schedule dial.** Apple Support: "Drag the curved slider to set Bedtime and Wake Up times. The slider will turn orange if the schedule doesn't meet your sleep goal." — [Apple Support 108906](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/108906). Earlier guides describe dragging a bed icon and a bell icon on the dial — [iPhone Life](https://iphonelife.com/content/how-to-use-bedtime-clock-ios-10); the Clock app exposes the same bed/alarm slider — [MacRumors how-to](https://www.macrumors.com/how-to/set-sleep-schedule-iphone-apple-watch). A 2025 Apple Community post calls it "difficult-to-use" and notes "others with manual dexterity issues may also have trouble" — [Apple Community thread](https://discussions-jp-prz.apple.com/thread/256134076).
- **Material 3 time picker (clock dial).** The guidelines page exists but is JavaScript-rendered; only the title was retrievable — [Material 3: Time pickers](https://m3.material.io/components/time-pickers/guidelines) (gap: specifics unverified).
- **Activity rings on iPhone/Watch.** Three nested rings, fixed colours and meanings; Apple forbids re-use for other data — [Apple HIG: Activity rings](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/activity-rings). Google Fit later adopted ring-style Move Minutes/Heart Points — [MacRumors forum](https://forums.macrumors.com/threads/google-fit-updates-with-apple-watch-like-rings-that-track-move-minutes-and-heart-points.2133393/page-2).
- **Carrot Weather.** Apple Design Award winner; watch app with four data slots and a live preview while editing — [Crosley design guide (JA)](https://blakecrosley.com/ja/guides/design/carrot-weather); [MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/carrot-weather-adds-powerful-redesigned-apple-watch-app/).
- **Oura.** Readiness score 0–100 ("85 or above is optimal") shown as a ring; the late-2025 redesign dropped card layouts for a more open style — [Oura blog: Readiness score](https://ouraring.com/blog/readiness-score/); [9to5Google (not fetched)](https://9to5google.com/2025/10/20/oura-app-redesign/). Garmin Body Battery glance: value plus recent-hours graph — [Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/forerunner165series/EN-SG/GUID-4CC69588-C506-4A49-8F34-662B56ADD18A-6341.html).
- **Nest-style ring on glass (precedent for combining set-point and consequence).** Nest shows the target temperature at the ring position and the current temperature as a separate marker, with a press on the ring to switch modes — [Cooper Hewitt](https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2014/11/12/drawings-for-nest-thermostat/); [Nest patents](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20120131504).

### Inferences
- The sleep-schedule dial is the closest shipped analogue to Someday's core: two inputs (bedtime, wake) on one ring, one derived output (hours of sleep) rendered as the arc between them, and an honesty cue (orange when the goal fails) that fires *during* the drag. Someday's equivalent: stop age and plan-to age as two handles on a life-span ring; the arc between them is "how long it lasts"; its fill is confidence; it turns from accent to warning the moment the plan fails in >X% of futures.
- The dexterity complaint about Apple's dial is the main accessibility risk of any ring input; the mitigation is already in the HIG (back every rotary interaction with touch/keyboard: tap a handle to focus it, then arrow keys ±1 year, and a numeric field on long-press for screen readers).
- iOS drums show that adjacent drums (one per parameter) read as *one* picker when they share a centre line and a single highlight band — the "one object" test passes even with three or four drums, but only when they are flush and share one read-off line. That supports a drum-based candidate (which the owner liked) provided the six drums are flush and read against one line, and it explains why the earlier two-ring cryptex felt like "too much hardware" (two separate objects, each with its own frame).
- Rings scale poorly past three nested bands (Apple stopped at three and fixed their meanings); so a six-input instrument should not be six concentric rings. Six *segments* of one ring (pie/marking-menu geometry, ≤8 items) is the evidenced layout.
- No shipped product found puts six independent parameters on one dial — Someday would be doing something new here, which is both the point of the brief and a reason to prototype the gesture early.

- Material 3 time-picker specifics (dial sizes, hour→minute focus change, accessibility) could not be read (JS-rendered page).
- Things, Fantastical and Citymapper non-form interactions were not researched in this pass (Things/Fantastical are also assigned to the spatial/3D researcher); no credible source was gathered.
- Nike Run Club/Strava goal rings: no design source found; omitted rather than guessed.
- No Oura design critique from a credible outlet was fetched.

## Cross-cutting notes for the report writer

- Strongest, most directly transferable evidence (in order): (1) Apple HIG Digital Crown rules — visible change on every turn, update speed tracks hand speed, back rotary with touch; (2) Kurtenbach & Buxton — ≤8 radial items, on-axis placement; (3) Garmin trend vector — a 6-second projection line as the mid-gesture answer cue; (4) Apple sleep-schedule dial — two handles, arc = derived value, orange = goal failed; (5) Nest — click per hashmark, pitch encodes direction; (6) Fujifilm/Rams — settings legible at rest, colour binds control to scale.
- Strongest cautions: Taycan over-configurability; Elektron shared-encoder ambiguity; touchscreen rotaries being hard without a large radius or a bound scale (Mixxx; tangible knobs 20% faster); Apple's own dial drawing dexterity complaints; NASA's finding that trend cues cost secondary-task attention (so: one trend cue only).
- Build-relevant numbers: ≥2 pt strokes at watch size (≈3 px phone); open gauges span 8→4 o'clock (≈240°); bezel text ≤180°; radial items ≤8 with <10% error; half-pie learning ≈5 minutes; tangible vs virtual knob ≈20% speed gap; pie vs linear ≈15% speed gap.
