# Spatial, 3D, rotational and zoomable interfaces for a one-screen phone-browser instrument

Scope: field 2 of the brief (spatial and 3D interfaces), researched for the Someday home screen: six input groups that must feel like one object you turn, flip, zoom or rotate, with deeper views reached by moving through space rather than scrolling. Everything below was examined directly unless marked "search excerpt only" or listed under Gaps. Apple HIG pages were read from Apple's own `.md`/JSON endpoints because the HTML pages render client-side. UK English throughout.

A quick legend used in the cited findings: **encodes** = the depth/rotation carries information (state, time, magnitude, hierarchy); **decorates** = it carries none.

---

## Key question 1: visionOS spatial windows and ornaments, watchOS Smart Stack and the Digital Crown, iOS Dynamic Island: what spatial grammar transfers to a flat phone screen?

### Takeaway
Apple's three spatial systems share one grammar that transfers cleanly to a flat phone: a single primary object; controls that float *in front of* it on a separate z-layer and stay put while content changes; depth reserved for hierarchy (what is in front is what matters now); a continuous physical control (the Crown) that inspects data rather than navigates lists; and a "morph, don't swap" rule where compact → expanded keeps relative placement so the eye never loses the element. None of these systems rotates the world, and Apple explicitly warns against letting people rotate a virtual world.

### Cited Findings

**visionOS ornaments (controls float in front of the object, and stay put)**
- "An ornament floats in a plane that's parallel to its associated window and slightly in front of it along the z-axis. If the associated window moves, the ornament moves with it, maintaining its relative position; if the window's contents scroll, the controls or information in the ornament remain unchanged." — [Apple HIG, Ornaments (JSON source)](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/ornaments.json)
- "Consider using an ornament to present frequently needed controls or information in a consistent location that doesn't clutter the window." … "In general, keep an ornament visible." … "Aim to keep an ornament's width the same or narrower than the width of the associated window." … "Consider using borderless buttons in an ornament" (background is glass, so a border is unnecessary). — [Apple HIG, Ornaments](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/ornaments.json)
- WWDC23 "Design for spatial user interfaces": ornaments sit outside the window at the bottom with depth and **overlap the window by 20 points**; sheets hold the window's z-position so the window "pushes back" behind them; tap targets at least **60×60 pt**; 44 pt buttons need at least 8 pt around them; buttons in stacks at least 16 pt; "use cross-dissolve, not sliding, when pushing a view onto a navigation stack"; "use wider layouts rather than taller ones, since side-to-side neck movement is easier than up-and-down." — [WWDC Notes, session 10076](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10076-design-for-spatial-user-interfaces/)
- Ornaments are "peripheral by design"; an ornament that "demands central attention (a flashing color, animated motion) defeats the purpose." Anti-patterns: the "panel app" (an iPad app with no volume, space or ornament), "fake 3D" (a volume filled with flat planes), and toolbars placed inside content, which "reads as iPad-on-Vision." Volumes are for "look at this 3D thing"; windows "behave most like iPad." — [Blake Crosley, visionOS Spatial Patterns](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/visionos-spatial-patterns)

**visionOS depth rules (depth = hierarchy, and only where it adds value)**
- "Use depth to communicate hierarchy. Depth helps an object appear to stand out from surrounding content, making it more noticeable. People also tend to notice changes in depth: for example, when a sheet appears over a window, the window recedes along the z-axis." — [Apple HIG, Spatial layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/spatial-layout.md)
- "In general, avoid adding depth to text. Text that appears to hover above its background is difficult to read." … "Make sure depth adds value … Depth is great for visually separating large, important elements … but it may not work as well on small objects." … "People need to refocus their eyes to perceive each difference in depth, and doing so too often or quickly can be tiring." — [Apple HIG, Spatial layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/spatial-layout.md)
- Interactive elements: "place multiple, regular-size buttons so their centers are at least 60 points apart, leaving 16 points or more of space between them." — [Apple HIG, Spatial layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/spatial-layout.md)
- "In general, avoid letting people rotate a virtual world. When a virtual world rotates, the experience typically upsets people's sense of stability, even when they control the rotation and the movement is subtle." … "Consider giving people a stationary frame of reference." … "Avoid showing objects that oscillate in a sustained way … around 0.2 Hz." … "Consider using fades when you need to relocate an object." — [Apple HIG, Motion (visionOS section)](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion.md)
- General motion rule: "Add motion purposefully … Gratuitous or excessive animation can distract people and may make them feel disconnected or physically uncomfortable." "Make motion optional … avoid using it as the only way to communicate important information." "Let people cancel motion." — [Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion.md)

**Digital Crown and Smart Stack (a continuous control that inspects data)**
- "Starting with watchOS 10, the Digital Crown takes on an elevated role as the primary input for navigation … people turn the Digital Crown to view widgets in the Smart Stack." — [Apple HIG, Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown.md)
- "Consider using the Digital Crown to inspect data in contexts where navigation isn't necessary … in World Clock, turning the Digital Crown advances the time of day at a selected location, allowing people to compare various times of day to their current time." — [Apple HIG, Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown.md)
- "Provide visual feedback in response to Digital Crown interactions … If you don't provide visual feedback, people are likely to assume that turning the Digital Crown has no effect." "Update your interface to match the speed with which people turn the Digital Crown … Avoid updating content at a rate that makes it difficult for people to select values." Default "linear haptic detents — or taps — as people turn the Digital Crown a specific distance"; turn them off "if the default detents don't match your app's animation." "When anchoring interactions to the Digital Crown, also be sure to back them up with corresponding touch screen interactions." — [Apple HIG, Digital Crown](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/digital-crown.md)
- Smart Stack: "a scrollable stack of widgets, accessed with an upward turn of the digital crown"; users "are expected to spend no more than 10 seconds with a glanceable widget"; six recommended layouts including "primarily numerical or a single important word" and "a chart"; dark material background with white text by default; backgrounds "can be dynamic to … convey information, such as … a red/green performance indicator." — [WWDC Notes, session 10309](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10309-design-widgets-for-the-smart-stack-on-apple-watch/)

**Dynamic Island (morph, don't swap; the shape *is* the state)**
- Three presentations: compact (two elements either side of the camera, "when only one Live Activity is active"), minimal ("up to three … One appears attached to the Dynamic Island, while a second (and third …) appear detached"), expanded ("When people touch and hold"). — [Apple HIG, Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities.md)
- "Maintain the relative placement of elements to create a coherent layout between presentations. The expanded presentation is an enlarged version of the compact or minimal presentation. Ensure information and layouts expand predictably." — [Apple HIG, Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities.md)
- "Live Activities use system and custom animations with a maximum duration of two seconds." "During the transition to a new layout, preserve as much of the existing layout as possible by animating existing elements to their new positions rather than removing and animating them back in." "Try to avoid overlapping elements." — [Apple HIG, Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities.md)
- "Use consistent margins and concentric placement … use ContainerRelativeShape to match the corner radius." "Ensure text is easy to read. Use large, heavier-weight text — a medium weight or higher." "adapt your compact and minimal presentations to fit inside a 28pt-diameter circle." Compact element 52.33×36.67 pt on a 393×852 screen; expanded 371×84–160 pt. — [Apple HIG, Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities.md)
- "Dynamically change the height … When there's less information to show, reduce the height … When more information becomes available, increase the height." — [Apple HIG, Live Activities](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/live-activities.md)
- WWDC23 10194: "Use of animation and continuously updating data here makes it feel more alive"; "Be concentric with its shape"; the minimal view should not be "just a logo" — "find a way to convey information even in this tiny state." — [WWDC Notes, session 10194](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10194-design-dynamic-live-activities/)
- Alan Dye: the island "didn't need to be a fixed size … we started to play with scale, and that unlocked a lot for us"; goal was "creating this really clear indication of what's happening across the system." Craig Federighi traces it to "an animation system that felt very physical and very direct." — [Cool Hunting, Dye and Federighi on Dynamic Island](https://coolhunting.com/tech/apples-alan-dye-and-craig-federighi-on-dynamic-island/)
- Re-creations on the web use SVG path morphing with a spring, or a Skia "metaball" with a hidden mock island behind the detaching element as a "center of gravity". — [Oliver Pan, Dynamic Island clone in React/Framer Motion](https://oliverpan.vercel.app/blog/building-ios-dynamic-island); [dev.to, Dynamic Island liquid animation with React Native Skia](https://dev.to/willkre/dynamic-island-liquid-animation-with-react-native-skia-5aeg)
- Critique: a 2025 article argues the island's animation quality has declined and the metaball merge is now "absent or poorly rendered" (secondary, opinion). — [iPhoneSoft](https://iphonesoft.fr/2025/03/16/qualite-animations-dynamic-island-baisse-deux-ans)

### Inferences
- For Someday, the transferable grammar is: one object centre-stage; a *stationary* ornament layer (the headline £/month, the confidence) that never moves while the object underneath is manipulated; depth used only to say "this is the layer you are acting on now" (the active input group comes forward, the others recede), never on text.
- Apple's "do not rotate the world" rule is the strongest argument against the rejected cryptex: the user rotated a large structure that carried the whole screen. Rotation is safe when it is a *small object within a stationary frame* (a dial, a drum under a fixed reticle) with the frame, background and headline fixed.
- The Dynamic Island's "expand predictably, preserve relative placement" rule gives a ready test for the deeper views: a band chart that grows out of the confidence pill, keeping the pill's position, reads as one object; a view that slides in from the side does not.
- The Digital Crown's World Clock pattern (turn = scrub a variable, display updates at the speed of the turn) is a direct model for "the answer moves while the hand is still on the control", and its detent guidance (turn detents off when they fight the animation) tells us when to use web vibration.

### Gaps
- Apple publishes no z-offset distance for ornaments beyond "slightly in front" and the 20 pt overlap figure in the WWDC talk.
- Apple publishes no animation curves or durations for the Dynamic Island morph beyond the two-second ceiling; the uxdesign.cc "four principles" article is paywalled and was not read.
- I did not find Apple Watch Ultra-specific guidance distinct from watchOS generally.

## Key question 2: WebGL "object as interface" award sites 2022–2026: which encode data, which decorate, and what do they cost on a phone?

### Takeaway
The award circuit has shifted hard to 3D (one third-party tally: 23% of Awwwards SOTD winners "immersive 3D" in 2024, 41% in 2025, 61% in Q1 2026, with Three.js the top framework), but almost all of the winning "object" sites are scroll-driven narrative or drag-to-orbit decoration, not instruments: the object does not encode the user's data. Published mobile performance figures for these sites essentially do not exist; the best available evidence is the Awwwards jury sub-scores (e.g. Igloo Inc: Accessibility 6.6/10 versus Animations 9.6/10) and anecdotal Safari crashes.

- Jam3's FWA-100 WebGL build write-up exists (Amélie Rosser) but was not opened. — [Medium, Jam3](https://medium.com/@Jam3/how-we-built-a-playful-webgl-experience-for-100-fwa-wins-12262265548d)

### Inferences
- Across every award object site examined, the manipulable thing is the *brand's* object (planet, product, globe of offices). In none does rotating or dragging change a number that belongs to the user. That is the gap Someday can occupy: an award-grade object whose geometry is the plan.
- The jury pattern (Animations 9.6, Accessibility 6.6) is the risk profile of this genre. Any Someday instrument must be built with the DOM/ARIA layer first and the 3D as a presentation of it, or it will inherit the same accessibility score.
- Scroll-driven narrative is the dominant idiom (87% of the index) and is exactly what the brief forbids; the drag-to-orbit idiom is the closest cousin to the brief, and it only works if the orbit angle *means* something (e.g. angle = stop age).

### Gaps
- No measured phone frame rates or load sizes were found for any SOTY site (KPR, Lusion v3, Igloo Inc). The Codrops interview with Adrián Gubrica on WebGL optimisation returned HTTP 403.
- FWA Site of the Year winners for 2024–2025 could not be confirmed from accessible sources; thefwa.com was not reachable through search in a form that named them.
- Page 2 of the Awwwards WebGL collection (14 more items) was not read.

## Key question 3: product configurators (Tesla, Porsche, Apple Watch Studio, Nike By You): how do they expose many parameters around one object without a form?

### Takeaway
The best configurators separate *what you are changing* (a single, always-visible object) from *the choice* (a small set of swatches or a slider directly beneath or around it), and they are ruthless about rendering cost: Tesla and Porsche's default views are pre-rendered image swaps that load in under two seconds on mobile, with true 3D offered only on demand to the ~5% who ask. Live 3D (Nike, Fender, McLaren) uses Babylon.js or a WebGL2 canvas with Draco-compressed glTF and drag-to-rotate; nobody uses a form, but nobody fuses the control into the object either.

### Cited Findings
- Tesla Model 3: "pre-rendered image swap in a React single-page app; no canvas or 3D engine"; picking colour/wheel/interior swaps a studio photo; images from a CDN; "the page loads in under two seconds on a mobile connection." — [Podify teardown of 12 configurators](https://dev.to/podifai/we-reverse-engineered-12-product-configurators-heres-whats-actually-under-the-hood-12ld)
- Porsche 911: pre-rendered by default (46 studio photos per colour), an "Open 360° View" button launches a separate 3D viewer on demand; "designed to work on slow connections, such as a dealer's iPad with 3G"; 3D "aimed at about 5% of visitors." — [Podify teardown](https://dev.to/podifai/we-reverse-engineered-12-product-configurators-heres-whats-actually-under-the-hood-12ld)
- Porsche's newer Cayenne EV configurator renders photoreal 3D and 2D in the cloud on "MHP's Elastic Content Platform and the Porsche Rendering Solution from Porsche Digital" (news report). — [eCommerceNews NZ](https://ecommercenews.co.nz/story/porsche-debuts-cloud-3d-configurator-for-cayenne-evs)
- Nike By You: Babylon.js with Draco-compressed glTF; "drag to rotate the shoe; color changes update the mesh live." Fender Mod Shop: Babylon.js assembled from five .glb parts. McLaren: InfinityRT on a WebGL2 canvas, moved away from server-side Unreal; "rotate the car in a 3D garage." Ferrari 296: Unreal Engine 5 pixel streaming, "$1.00–$1.50/hr per A10G instance." Tylko: WebGL parametric engine that "rebuilds geometry as sliders change." Vans Customs: 2D canvas compositor "designed to run smoothly on a five-year-old Android phone." — [Podify teardown](https://dev.to/podifai/we-reverse-engineered-12-product-configurators-heres-whats-actually-under-the-hood-12ld)
- Nike's own framing of the 3D builder: it shifts customisation "from a fixed state to a dynamic experience", with zoom for close-up detail. A separate glossary claims Nike uses "cleverly layered 2D images"; the two conflict and neither names the engine. — [just-style](https://www.just-style.com/news/nike-to-launch-3d-sneaker-customisation-platform/); [thedigitalbunch glossary](https://www.thedigitalbunch.com/glossary/2d-configurator)
- Apple Watch Studio: at launch (2019) it let shoppers combine case and band in one interface, test combinations, then press "I'm done" before confirming size and cellular. No UX analysis of the live page was found; an open-source clone describes "interactive sliders for size, case, and band selection" with Framer Motion. — [TrendHunter](https://www.trendhunter.com/trends/apple-watch-studio); [Apple Watch Studio clone (GitHub, via ecosyste.ms)](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fdevdignesh%2Fapple-watch-studio-clone)
- Demodern's smart "Highend 3D Car Configurator in WebGL" (Daimler) used WebGL so users can "configure equipment and options on the car." — [Demodern](https://demodern.com/projects/smart-cc)

### Inferences
- The configurator lesson for Someday is a two-tier render budget: the always-on instrument should be as cheap as an image swap (CSS/SVG/2D canvas), and the expensive thing (if any) must be opt-in and lazy. Tesla and Porsche effectively concede that true 3D is not worth its load cost for a first impression.
- Tylko is the closest analogue to Someday: a slider regenerates the object's geometry. Translate: dragging "stop age" should regenerate the plan's shape (the wealth curve, the confidence arc), not swap a picture.
- Apple Watch Studio's single "I'm done" moment is the right model for the brief's "the moment it is complete": one confirm, not a stepper.

### Gaps
- No primary source for Apple Watch Studio's interaction design or technology; apple.com/shop/studio is client-rendered and was not fetched.
- Tesla's configurator UX has no serious published analysis; the Monzani and Korean review articles surfaced in search were not opened.

## Key question 4: zoomable user interfaces (Pad++, Prezi, Figma, Muse, Magic Ink): how is depth-by-zoom used to hold detail without scrolling, and what are the pitfalls?

### Takeaway
ZUIs hold detail by letting scale stand in for hierarchy (semantic zoom: the same object shows more as it grows), and they work when the transition is continuous, interruptible and anchored ("you never lose your orientation", Muse). The well-documented pitfalls are desert fog (zooming into featureless space with no cues), seasickness from unconstrained zoom-and-spin (Prezi), and inconsistency when some objects do not obey the spatial model. Bret Victor's position reframes the brief: for information software "all interaction is essentially navigation around a data space", so the ideal is to need almost no interaction at all.

### Cited Findings
- Pad++ (Bederson & Meyer): "The entire metaphor is based on animation. If the system becomes slow and jerky, the metaphor dies. Frame rates of anything less than 10 frames per second are unacceptable." Goal "frame times of at most 100 milliseconds (ms), or 10 frames per second"; "refinements are interruptible … if an event is found on the input queue, the renderer finishes rendering the back buffer at LOR 0". — [Bederson & Meyer, Implementing a Zooming User Interface: Experience Building Pad++ (PDF)](https://mrl.cs.nyu.edu/publications/zooming/zooming.pdf)
- Jul & Furnas, "Critical Zones in Desert Fog: Aids to Multiscale Navigation", UIST 1998, 223 citations (Semantic Scholar); desert fog is an environment with no navigational cues — "an island of information surrounded by desert fog"; the French course deck adds that objects are "present but hidden because they are too small to see", and proposes critical zones and residue as aids (search excerpts and slide text only; the ACM page returned 403). — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/288392.288578?fields=title,abstract,year,authors,citationCount); [Stasko course deck (search excerpt)](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/11s/Talks/zoom.pdf); [LIRMM deck (PDF, French)](https://www.lirmm.fr/~mountaz/Ens/Dea/zoom.pdf)
- Bederson 2011, "The promise of zoomable user interfaces", Behaviour & Information Technology 30(6) 853–866: ZUIs address "the problem of having more information than fits on screen"; elements appear in everyday computing but "the broader vision of a zoomable desktop has not materialized"; includes design guidelines and "a cautionary tale" (search excerpts; the PDF returned 503). — [DOI 10.1080/0144929X.2011.586724](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144929X.2011.586724)
- Muse (Ink & Switch): zoom was chosen "to leverage users' innate spatial thinking, visual processing, and hand-eye coordination"; "the interface responds instantly" and settles "at the nearest stable zoom level when the gesture ends"; "there are no discrete navigation jumps"; "you never lose your orientation"; users can "peek" by zooming in and back out in one gesture; pinch and double-tap were "deliberately borrow[ed]" from maps. Nested boards: "Just scaling down the board would make an inner board hard to distinguish", so they added "a slight haze over distant boards, so that they appear darker the further away they are." Findings: "Users quickly learned the interface and reported it made intuitive sense"; pitfalls: "text cards also don't follow the spatial model … reduces consistency"; "A phone is probably too small to legibly render a whole board." — [Ink & Switch, Muse](https://www.inkandswitch.com/muse/)
- Prezi motion sickness is widely reported but not formally studied: "especially when viewed on large screens, this extreme use of zooming and spinning has been known to literally cause actual motion sickness" (Packt guide); a presentation writer says Prezi "gives you the unique new power of making your audience seasick"; a 2026 review aggregator notes nausea/dizziness complaints, "design-dependent". — [Packt, Mastering Prezi](https://packtpub.com/en-ec/product/mastering-prezi-for-business-presentations-update-9781782175094/chapter/1-understanding-the-prezi-frame-of-mind-1/section/common-prezi-mistakes-ch01lvl1sec06); [Claus Wilke](https://clauswilke.com/blog/2013/08/21/my-gripes-with-prezis-and-some-suggestions-for-making-good-prezi-presentations/); [checkthat.ai reviews](https://checkthat.ai/brands/prezi-inc/reviews)
- Figma: "we essentially made 'a browser inside a browser.' Our design editor is powered by WebGL and WebAssembly, with some of the user interface implemented in Typescript and React" (Figma quote reproduced in a secondary article); GPU matrix transforms make zoom cost independent of point count where 2D canvas cost grows with it. — [Medium, Figma is a game engine](https://medium.com/@nike_thana/figma-is-a-game-engine-not-a-web-app-how-c-and-wasm-broke-the-react-ceiling-8ed991bea48f); [2D graphs WebGL benchmark](https://2dgraphs.netlify.com/)
- Bret Victor, Magic Ink: information software "is graphic design"; "all interaction is essentially navigation around a data space"; navigation is "pure excise" (after Cooper); when interaction is unavoidable use "graphical manipulation", "relative navigation" (correct a predicted starting point) and "tight feedback loops" where each action visibly changes the graphic; the train timetable becomes a time–distance graph where a delay is "drawing a new line"; the BART widget needs no interaction most of the time and configuration is edited "within a sentence rather than a dialog box." — [Bret Victor, Magic Ink](http://worrydream.com/MagicInk/)

### Inferences
- Semantic zoom is the ZUI idea that transfers: the confidence pill *is* the band chart at a larger scale; the headline *is* the year-by-year at a larger scale. That satisfies "nothing said twice" because each deeper view is the same object, bigger.
- Desert fog is avoided automatically if zoom is quantised to named levels (home → bands → years) and every level keeps a fixed anchor (the headline number). Muse's "settle at the nearest stable zoom level" plus Apple's "expand predictably" together specify this.
- Muse's haze (distant = darker) is a cheap, encoded use of depth: in Someday, unfilled input groups can sit "further away" (dimmer, smaller) and come forward as they are set, which is the owner's night-to-dawn instinct expressed as depth.
- Victor's "relative navigation" argues for pre-filled defaults (State Pension age, typical pot) that the user corrects, rather than empty fields; combined with "tight feedback loops" this is the brief's "setting an input and seeing the answer are the same gesture."

### Gaps
- Neither Jul & Furnas nor Bederson 2011 could be read in full (403/503); findings are from abstracts, slide decks and search excerpts.
- No controlled study quantifying Prezi/ZUI cybersickness was found; the evidence is practitioner and user reports.
- Muse's exact card-scaling rules across zoom levels were not documented in the sources read.

## Key question 5: globes and 3D maps as instruments (Google Earth, Apple Maps, Flightradar24, Windy, Fathom, GraphCast): how do they fuse a continuous control with live data, and what does it cost on a phone?

### Takeaway
The instrument globes fuse control and data by making the rotation itself select the datum (what is under the reticle is what you are reading) and by moving per-frame work to the GPU. The only measured phone figure found is a Cesium/GraphCast demo at "buttery-smooth 60 FPS" on a 2022 iPhone SE, achieved by displacing vertices in a vertex shader from a texture rather than touching geometry on the CPU; the same project hit out-of-memory crashes when it loaded a 40-band texture at once. Full Earth engines explicitly warn they are "hardware intensive"; Windy historically used a globe only at low zoom and let slow devices turn it off.

### Cited Findings
- Fathom "No Ceilings" mobile globe: "Sliding a finger across the screen rotates the globe", pinch zooms, swiping across the top selects a topic; numeric indicators use a continuous green→blue gradient (Rwanda 56.3% women in parliament vs US 17.8%); built with Three.js on WebGL; WebGL chosen to "maximize performance on mobile devices with low powered processors" (no benchmark). — [Fathom notebook](https://fathom.info/notebook/11437)
- GraphCast on iPhone with Cesium: "buttery-smooth 60 FPS" on a 2022 iPhone SE; custom GLSL vertex shader reads pressure from a texture and displaces vertices each frame; 1440×721 global grid; the 40-band GeoTIFF caused an "Out-of-Memory (OOM) error" until split into 40 single-band textures preloaded sequentially; Cesium's native terrain path "overwhelmed the CPU when updating geometry each frame." — [dev.to, GraphCast on iPhone with Cesium](https://dev.to/ryousuke_wayama_f134a69e4/how-we-animated-graphcasts-global-weather-predictions-in-real-time-on-an-iphone-using-cesium--3o0m)
- Flightradar24 3D view: "3D View is hardware intensive. It's designed to work best on newer computers and modern browsers … older hardware (especially older graphics cards) is generally unsupported"; built on WebGL with a Cesium globe and Mapbox imagery; aircraft below 500 m cast a sun-position shadow; detail loads on demand with zoom. Moved from Google Earth plugin to Cesium in 2016 "to be viewed on a wider array of devices, without a plugin." — [Flightradar24 blog](https://www.flightradar24.com/blog/inside-flightradar24/exploring-the-new-flightradar24-3d-view/); [Cesium blog 2016](https://cesium.com/blog/2016/04/20/flightradar24/)
- Google Earth web: C++ compiled to WebAssembly; multithreading needs SharedArrayBuffer (removed after Spectre, restored in Chrome via Site Isolation); "running work on a background thread has shown a clear improvement"; Safari lacked full WebGL2 at the time so Earth did not run; "Earth may fail to start on memory-constrained devices." No mobile-browser notes. — [web.dev case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/earth-webassembly)
- Windy (then Windyty 5.2): "From zoom 0 to 4 Windyty is displayed on 3D globe. Higher zooms use old Mercator projection. If you are fan of Mercator, or have really slow device, 3D mode can be disabled in Settings menu." A later community thread suggests the 3D option was removed ("It was available until last October"); current status unconfirmed. — [Windyty 5.2 announcement](https://community.windytv.com/topic/65/windyty-5-2-alias-3d-is-here?); [Windy community thread](https://community.windy.com/topic/40960/zoom-out-world-view)
- Cesium community experience with heavy data on mobile globes: "memory limits, terrible framerates, and inevitable browser crashes are common" (search excerpt). — [dev.to GraphCast article, context](https://dev.to/ryousuke_wayama_f134a69e4/how-we-animated-graphcasts-global-weather-predictions-in-real-time-on-an-iphone-using-cesium--3o0m)

### Inferences
- The globe pattern that transfers is "the thing under the reticle is the reading": rotate a ring of years under a fixed marker and the headline becomes the figure for that year. The data is fused to the rotation because position *is* the query.
- The GraphCast technique (per-frame change expressed as a texture/uniform sampled in a shader, geometry fixed) is the way to animate 2,000 Monte Carlo paths or a band fan at 60 fps on a 2022-class phone: upload the engine's output once as a texture, drive the display with one uniform (the scrubbed year or stop age).
- Memory, not fill rate, was the failure mode on iPhone. Any WebGL approach for Someday must budget textures carefully; iOS Safari also has a per-page memory ceiling (see Q7).

### Gaps
- No performance data for Apple Maps 3D (native, not web) and nothing current on Windy's globe implementation.
- Google Earth's current mobile-browser behaviour was not documented in the sources found.

## Key question 6: rotation as navigation in games (Monument Valley, The Room, Fez, Alto's, Tetris Effect): evidence of learnability, and what makes rotation meaningful?

### Takeaway
The evidence that rotation is learnable in seconds is practical rather than experimental: ustwo built Monument Valley inside a UX studio, tested "early and regularly" with testers "from a plethora of different backgrounds, skill sets and ages", kept input to tap/drag, and shipped with effectively no tutorial; Fez taught a 2D section first and then let rotation reveal paths. Rotation feels meaningful when it changes what is *possible* (a path connects, a lock opens) and when the input is forgiving of imprecise 2D gestures on 3D things; it feels gimmicky when "most solutions are just one or two rotations away" and when the whole world turns (Fez players report motion sickness with no option to reduce it).

### Cited Findings
- GDC Europe 2014, Ken Wong, "Designing Monument Valley: Less Game, More Experience": design shaped by Escher and by "being embedded in a UX studio"; the team "traded challenging puzzles for an aesthetic journey", kept "short but sublime"; "every screen was a piece of art." — [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1021380/Designing-Monument-Valley-Less)
- Dan Gray (ustwo) on process: the wider studio's "proper user testing" was the team's "main boon"; "Being able to borrow testers from a plethora of different backgrounds, skill sets and ages"; feedback shaped "the user interactions specifically"; the game was "designed to be accessible to everyone, not just gamers." — [PocketGamer.biz, Stairway to heaven](http://www.pocketgamer.biz/stairway-to-heaven-the-making-of-monument-valley/)
- Ken Wong (search excerpt attributed to his own write-up; full article not loadable): "touch controls can be very ambiguous, especially when rotating 3D objects using 2D gestures. We found that everybody touches the screen slightly differently"; "touch controls are very imprecise, you have to be very forgiving"; "By testing early and regularly we would constantly refine our puzzles and get a better understanding of how people interacted with the game." — [Creative Bloq, The making of Monument Valley (search excerpt)](https://www.creativebloq.com/computer-arts/making-monument-valley-71412213)
- Game Developer: Windosill as the model — "The game just trusts that beautiful animations and intuitive controls are enough"; "Windosill doesn't require anything more than swiping to explore"; "We find you need to make a game wrong at least two or three times before you find the right path"; "Difficulty is not that important to the user's experience in Monument Valley." — [Game Developer, When quality comes before making money](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/when-quality-comes-before-making-money-developing-i-monument-valley-i-)
- Lea Schönfelder (MV2): the tutorial "effectively doesn't exist"; the studio frames progression as surprise rather than challenge (search excerpt). — [Killscreen, A Monument to Experience](https://www.killscreen.com/lea-schonfelder/)
- The Room (Fireproof), Mark Hamilton: "The original game idea came from thinking about what kind of movements and manipulations were best suited to the touch screen, then working out what kind of game we could make from that"; "it took a couple of months of trial and error to get them working the way they are now"; goal to "make the game feel tactile and tangible, something not always easy with just a glass screen"; "By tapping, swiping and sliding (and a couple of other neat gestures)". Barry Meade (search excerpt): the puzzle box was chosen because it "used the touch interface really well"; a lock might have "10 different things you can do to open it or 100 or 150." — [TheSixthAxis, Mark Hamilton interview](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2013/04/26/talking-the-room-with-fireproofs-mark-hamilton/); [GamerHorizon, Barry Meade interview](https://gamerhorizon.com/2014/08/19/room-interview-fireproof-studios-barry-meade/)
- Fez: rotation "bend[s] space" a quarter turn at a time; the critic argues that with a short jump and only two rotation directions "most solutions are just one or two rotations away", so challenges are shallow; the game lacked "appropriate feedback/tutorials." Phil Fish made the first part strictly 2D to teach platforming before rotation, and cut content unrelated to "the core mechanic of rotation"; players report motion sickness and no option to reduce rotation (Wikipedia summary of reviews). — [Game Developer, The Dimensions of Fez](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-dimensions-of-fez); [Wikipedia, Fez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fez_(video_game))
- Alto's: "an elegant one-touch trick system at the heart of the series"; the team said "anything requiring more than one touch input didn't feel right" (search excerpts). — [Alto's Adventure press](https://www.altosadventure.com/press/spiritofthemountain); [PocketGamer interview](https://www.pocketgamer.com/altos-odyssey/interview-a-closer-look-at-team-altos-stunning-sequel-altos-odyssey)
- Tetris Effect Zone: grew from "one idea of not clearing completed lines immediately"; a meter fills as lines clear, activation slows time and holds pieces; reviewers say it "walks the fine line between assisting Tetris newcomers while offering more depth to seasoned vets" (search excerpts). — [Unreal Engine interview](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/developer-interviews/how-tetris-effect-became-a-modern-work-of-art); [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2018/06/25/how-tetris-effect-adds-a-new-strategic-layer-to-a-decades-old-formula)

### Inferences
- Monument Valley's rotation is learnable because it is *one* mechanic, visibly attached to a handle (a dial on the structure), applied to a small object inside a stationary frame, and because turning it immediately changes the picture. The cryptex failed the brief on the first and third counts (two rings, and the whole surface moved).
- "Forgiving" for Someday means: coarse angular quantisation (a few degrees per step), generous dead zones, snapping on release, and accepting both a drag on the object and a drag anywhere on the screen as the same rotation.
- Fez's critique (shallow because rotation changes little) is the test for gimmick: rotation must change the *answer*, not the view of the answer. Rotating to a different input group is navigation; rotating to a different stop age is manipulation. Only the latter earns the 3D.
- The Room's lesson is the number of affordances per object: an instrument with several things to do to the same object (turn, press, zoom) reads as physical and rewards exploration, as long as each does one thing.

### Gaps
- No experimental study measuring time-to-learn for rotation controls on touch was found in this field; the evidence is design-process testimony. (Fitts's-law/radial-vs-linear studies belong to the academic researcher's scope.)
- The GDC talk transcript and slides were not accessible; only the session description was read.
- The Creative Bloq article could not be loaded in full, so the Wong touch quotes rest on a search excerpt.

## Key question 7: web techniques for rotational/drum/radial controls on touch (R3F/drei, Theatre.js, Framer Motion 3D, Rive, CSS 3D, scroll-timeline, pointer capture) and iOS Safari pitfalls

### Takeaway
The toolchain has moved: `framer-motion-3d` is deprecated and does not support React 19; Theatre.js's R3F extension is alive but low-traffic (0.7.2, ~424 weekly downloads, docs last edited Feb 2024); drei's `PerformanceMonitor` gives a documented way to step device pixel ratio by measured fps; Rive's WebGL2 runtime is its recommended path but Canvas can win on mobile when blend modes are used; CSS scroll-driven animations shipped in Safari 26 with threaded support in 26.4; WebGL2 has been default in Safari since 15 and WebGPU since Safari 26. The iOS Safari pitfalls are concrete and documented: pointer capture leaked one event to the wrong element even after the 15.5 fix, `backdrop-filter` breaks `backface-visibility` and 3D-transformed children (several bugs 2022–2025), per-page memory limits cause crashes when 3D transforms and `will-change` promote too many layers, and `svh`/`dvh` were not distinguished in one 2023 bug.

### Cited Findings

**Libraries**
- drei `PerformanceMonitor`: collects average fps over `ms` (default 250) for `iterations` (10); `threshold` 0.75; `bounds` default `(r) => r > 90 ? [50, 90] : [50, 60]`; `flipflops` (default Infinity) then `onFallback` and the monitor "shuts down"; `factor` 0–1 with `step` 0.1; example toggles `dpr` between 1 and 2 starting at 1.5, or gradually via `onChange`. Children can call `usePerformanceMonitor`. — [drei docs, PerformanceMonitor](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/performances/performance-monitor)
- Community R3F mobile guidance (not official): cap DPR at 1–1.5 on mobile, `frameloop="demand"`, disable antialiasing, keep useFrame JS under ~4 ms, draw calls under ~50 on mobile, avoid allocating in `useFrame`, dispose geometries. A three.js forum thread warns that adjusting pixel ratio from measured fps "created a loop that got stuck." — [R3F performance skill page](https://www.skills.lc/Bbeierle12/Skill-MCP-Claude/bbeierle12-skill-mcp-claude-skills-r3f-performance-skill-md); [three.js discourse](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/changing-pixelratio-based-on-fps-good-or-bad-idea/34563)
- `framer-motion-3d` is deprecated ("no longer supported"), last version 12.4.13; discontinued and does not support React 19; the replacement `motion/react` does not work with three.js elements. — [npm, framer-motion-3d](https://npmjs.com/package/framer-motion-3d); [three.js discourse](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/r3f-and-animation-replace-motion-3d-and-declarative-approach/80663)
- `@theatre/r3f` 0.7.2, 424 weekly downloads; official docs page last edited 1 Feb 2024; earlier notes called it pre-release. — [npm, @theatre/r3f](https://npmjs.com/package/@theatre/r3f); [Theatre.js docs](https://www.theatrejs.com/docs/latest/extensions/react-three-fiber)
- Rive web: `@rive-app/webgl2` and `@rive-app/canvas` share one API; "@rive-app/webgl2 if you want the best rendering quality and performance in most cases"; without `WEBGL_shader_pixel_local_storage` it falls back to MSAA, where "any blend mode other than Normal forces the renderer to re-read the frame as it draws, and that cost adds up quickly on mobile browsers"; `@rive-app/canvas-lite` is smallest; the old `@rive-app/webgl` is deprecated after v2.37.0; instances must be deleted to avoid C++ leaks; a state machine name must be passed explicitly. — [Rive docs, Canvas vs WebGL](https://rive.app/docs/runtimes/web/canvas-vs-webgl.md); [Rive docs, web runtime](https://rive.app/docs/runtimes/web/)
- Rive vs Lottie size: vendors claim `.riv` is "10-15x smaller" than Lottie JSON; one independent-looking comparison says about one-tenth; Callstack cites a 24.37 KB Lottie recreated as ~2 KB Rive (from a Rive CEO tweet; unverified). — [Rive blog](https://rive.app/blog/rive-as-a-lottie-alternative); [LottieFiles blog](https://lottiefiles.com/blog/lottie-animations/lottiefiles-or-rive); [Callstack](https://callstack.com/blog/lottie-vs-rive-optimizing-mobile-app-animation)
- Open-source iOS-style drum pickers for the web: Kokonut UI wheel picker (React, drag, flick momentum, keyboard, uses `motion`); Mantine Picker (configurable `perspective`, `maxRotation`, `cylinderRadius`, inertia deceleration); OutSystems "iOS Inspired Infinity Wheel" (3D cylindrical projection with `preserve-3d`, touch velocity flick, snap to centre); `@uinstinct/svelte-wheel-picker` (spring inertia; cylindrical mode uses faux-3D `scaleY` instead of `rotateX`). — [21st.dev, Wheel Picker](https://21st.dev/@kokonutd/components/wheel-picker.md); [Mantine Picker README](https://unpkg.com/@gfazioli/mantine-picker@3.1.2/README.md); [OutSystems Forge](https://www.outsystems.com/forge/component-overview/23923/ios-inspired-infinity-wheel-o11); [svelte-wheel-picker](https://www.pkgstats.com/pkg:@uinstinct/svelte-wheel-picker)
- Codrops has no modern cylinder tutorial; the 2012 "3D Gallery with CSS3 and jQuery" is a three-panel perspective ring, and a Codrops-author repo includes a "Cylinder Text Rotation" driven by scroll. — [Codrops 3D Gallery](https://tympanus.net/codrops/?p=7643); [3d-text-animation-codrops repo](https://github.ddaodan.workers.dev/timmeulendijk/3d-text-animation-codrops)

**Platform support**
- WebGL 2.0 shipped in Safari 15 on macOS and iOS, giving all major browsers support; Safari's implementation runs on Metal. — [Khronos blog](https://www.khronos.org/blog/webgl-2-achieves-pervasive-support-from-all-major-web-browsers)
- WebGPU is on by default in Safari 26 (macOS, iOS, iPadOS, visionOS), shipped September 2025 (secondary sources; Apple release notes are client-rendered and were not readable). — [Cinevva news](https://app.cinevva.com/news/2025-09-15-safari-webgpu); [App Developer Magazine](https://appdevelopermagazine.com/WebGPU-in-iOS-26/)
- Scroll-driven animations (`animation-timeline`): Chrome/Edge 115, Safari 26; "Safari 26.4 added threaded scroll-driven animations, and Safari 26.5 fixed a batch of progress-accuracy and animation-play-state bugs"; Firefox still behind a flag as of 152 (June 2026); caniuse ~82.6% global. Browsers that do not understand `animation-timeline` ignore the line, so the layout must be correct with the animation stripped. — [Chrome Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/scroll-driven-animations?hl=en); [buildmvpfast blog](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/css-scroll-driven-animations-replace-js-2026)

**iOS Safari pitfalls**
- Pointer capture: WebKit bug 220196 "[iOS] #setPointerCapture does not dispatch pointer events outside element boundary"; fix shipped in Safari 15.5, but a reporter found that "if the capture is obtained during pointerdown, the next pointer event (either pointermove or pointerup) will still be dispatched to the wrong element." — [WebKit Bugzilla 220196](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=220196)
- `backdrop-filter` + 3D: an element with `backface-visibility:hidden` lost that behaviour when `backdrop-filter:blur(9px)` was added (iOS 15); bug 252181 (3D transform + backdrop-filter renders incorrectly; a 2025 comment says Tahoe is "much improved" but blur "still looks a little off"); bug 263273 (3D children vanish when given backdrop-filter; fixed 2024 as duplicate of 282682); bug 289149 (Safari 18.2/iOS 18: backdrop-filter not applied when the value comes from a custom property; reproduction disputed). — [webkit-unassigned, Dec 2022](https://lists.webkit.org/pipermail/webkit-unassigned/2022-December/1078432.html); [WebKit 252181](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=252181); [WebKit 263273](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=263273); [WebKit 289149](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=289149)
- Intersecting planes: a Firefox bug comment claims Safari handles intersecting planes correctly because CoreAnimation "splits intersecting planes into multiple pieces so that they can be sorted correctly" (unverified); WebKit 47125 shows Safari and Chrome historically disagreed on 3D sorting. — [Mozilla 689498](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=689498); [WebKit 47125](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=47125)
- Layer memory: "Safari has a hard limit on how much RAM a web page can consume … These [3D transforms and unnecessary will-change] were contributing to Safari crashing. Cutting down on these layers stopped our page from crashing"; `will-change` "promotes an element to its own GPU compositing layer … The cost is GPU memory, one texture per promoted layer"; it "affects everything that sits above it in z-order and physically overlaps with it"; on mobile the browser "may start evicting layers, which creates exactly the kind of jank you were trying to prevent"; `filter: blur(100px)` on several elements produced severe jank and white flashes on iPhone X while Android stayed smooth. — [Dev log: Debugging Safari, an ogre with layers](https://hacklines.com/en/editions/331993/click); [mspk, what will-change does to GPU memory](https://mspk.substack.com/p/what-will-change-actually-does-to); [dev.to, the will-change tax](https://dev.to/ouvarov/the-will-change-tax-how-a-600-kb-transform-hint-cost-me-5-mb-n26); [besthub, one CSS property slowed down iOS](https://besthub.dev/articles/why-one-css-property-slowed-down-ios-and-how-a-single-line-fixed-it-20c33877cc48)
- Viewport units: `svh/lvh/dvh` supported from iOS 15.4; WebKit 261185 (2023) reported "Mobile Safari is not distinguishing between the CSS units svh and dvh" and an engineer agreed it was unexpected; `dvh`, `svh` and `100vh` all stay the same when the keyboard opens, so use the Visual Viewport API. — [WebKit 261185](https://www2.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=261185); [dev.to, why dvh ignores the keyboard](https://dev.to/rl0425/why-css-dvh-ignores-the-mobile-keyboard-and-how-to-fix-it-31ao)
- Reduced motion for WebGL: read `prefers-reduced-motion` via `matchMedia`, cancel the rAF loop and show a static state, re-check on change; one React/Three example marks the canvas `aria-hidden`; a checklist item is whether auto-playing WebGL has a static fallback. — [Verdigris Reduced Motion Guide](https://design.verdigris.co/categories/animation/reduced-motion); [WeAreDevelopers, detecting reduced motion](https://www.wearedevelopers.com/magazine/accessibility-tricks-detecting-reduced-motion-in-one-line-of-javascript)

### Inferences
- For a React build in 2026 the viable stacks are: (a) CSS 3D (`rotateX` cylinder with `preserve-3d`) plus pointer events and hand-rolled flick physics, which the drum-picker libraries show is a solved problem; (b) R3F + drei with `PerformanceMonitor` stepping DPR 1→1.5→2 and `frameloop="demand"`; (c) Rive WebGL2 for a designed, state-machine instrument. Theatre.js and Framer Motion 3D should not be load-bearing.
- CSS 3D is the cheapest to make accessible (the faces are real DOM) but carries the Safari layer-memory risk; keep the number of simultaneously promoted faces small (one drum of ~7 visible faces, not six drums), never combine `backdrop-filter` with a 3D-transformed subtree, and avoid large blurs.
- For WebGL, the DOM must remain the source of truth: render inputs as real `<input type=range>`/`role=slider` elements (visually hidden or as the ornament layer) and let the canvas be `aria-hidden`; this also gives the reduced-motion fallback for free (the DOM state is the static view).
- The pointer-capture quirk means the rotation handler should be attached to a full-screen overlay during the drag (so the "wrong element" is still ours) rather than relying on capture.

### Gaps
- No vendor benchmarks were found for Rive WebGL2 vs Canvas on real phones, nor for drei `PerformanceMonitor` outcomes on specific devices.
- Apple's Safari 26 release notes could not be read (client-rendered); WebGPU and scroll-timeline support claims rest on secondary sources.
- No published measurement of CSS `preserve-3d` cylinder frame rates on a three-year-old phone was found.

### Inferences
- The patterns that satisfy "setting an input and seeing the answer are the same gesture" are the Crown-as-scrubber and globe-under-reticle families: one continuous rotation whose angle *is* the value, under a fixed reading. Everything else separates choosing from reading.
- The patterns that satisfy "six inputs feel like one object" without the cryptex's "everything said three times" are depth-as-hierarchy plus Muse's haze: one object, six faces or sectors at different depths/brightness, where distance encodes "not yet set" and coming forward encodes "being set" — so the night-to-dawn atmosphere the owner liked becomes a depth cue rather than a decoration.
- The patterns that satisfy "deeper views without scrolling" are semantic zoom with settle and the Dynamic Island morph: the confidence/band view is the same object at a larger scale, expanding predictably from its home position and collapsing back.
- Render budget by evidence: CSS 3D or 2D canvas for the always-on instrument (image-swap-class cost), WebGL only for the band fan/edge map and only via texture-driven shaders with DPR stepping; nothing heavier than GraphCast's technique has a measured 60 fps on a 2022 iPhone SE in the sources found.

### Gaps
- Published, device-named frame-rate measurements for *any* of the web patterns above are limited to the single GraphCast/Cesium figure; everything else is targets, anecdotes or jury sub-scores. The build should include its own measurement on a 2022–2023 mid-range phone.
- No example was found of an award-winning web object whose geometry encodes the viewer's own numbers; the nearest are Fathom's globe (public data) and Tylko (furniture dimensions).
