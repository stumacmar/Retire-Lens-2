# Data physicalisation, explorable explanations and uncertainty visualisation for Someday's one-screen home

Scope: how to encode uncertainty and time in a minimal, non-scrolling, possibly radial or 3D primary view on a phone; which encodings lay people read correctly in seconds; how to couple a live control to a live answer; and which visual grammars from data art carry a "futuristic, minimalist" feel without decoration. The home must show, at a glance, while the user is turning a control: the headline (£ a month after tax, today's money), how long the money lasts, the share of 2,000 simulated futures in which it holds, a wealth projection with good/base/poor bands, and the safe figure under Japan 1990 / 1970s stagflation / 2008-at-retirement.

Reading the brief (`docs/UX_RESEARCH_BRIEF.md` §1–3, §6): the home is scored on Different, One screen, One object, Live, Honest, Minimal, Legible in ten seconds, Buildable. Forms, wizards, steppers, carousels, chat and tile dashboards are off the table; a drum/dial/pie/orrery instrument with serif numerals and a meaningful background is what the owner wants more of.

## KQ1. Uncertainty visualisation research (Hullman, Kay, Correll, Padilla): which encodings improve lay decisions, which are misread, and what is known about animating uncertainty on small screens

### Takeaway
Frequency-based encodings (quantile dotplots, icon-array-style "N of 100" counts) and animated draws (HOPs) are the two families with the strongest evidence for lay audiences; intervals, bars with error bars, crisp-edged bands and anything with a hard boundary are the ones most reliably misread. Quantile dotplots were designed for small phone screens and improved real decisions to 97% of optimal; HOPs help most when people must compare two or three uncertain things and run acceptably at roughly 400 ms per frame, but there is no direct evidence about HOPs on a phone while the user is dragging a control.

### Cited Findings
- Hypothetical Outcome Plots (HOPs) show a finite set of individual random draws as an animation, letting viewers infer the distribution by "mental processes like counting and integration". In the 2015 experiment, HOPs produced much more accurate judgements than error bars and violin plots for questions about two or three quantities; for a single quantity's distribution, accuracy was similar across all three. — [Hullman, Resnick, Adar 2015, PLOS ONE (UW IDL page)](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops)
- In the 2018 follow-up, "untrained observers" could identify the correct underlying trend from weaker evidence when using HOPs than with error bars (Exp. 1) or static line ensembles (Exp. 2). HOPs ran at **400 ms per frame**; the authors suggest animation helps beyond frequency framing because it removes the visual clutter static ensembles create. — [Kale, Nguyen, Kay, Hullman 2018, InfoVis/TVCG (UW IDL page)](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops-trends); [PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2018-HOPsTrends-InfoVis.pdf)
- Kale, Kay & Hullman (InfoVis 2020 best paper) tested eight designs (95% intervals, HOPs, densities, quantile dotplots, each with and without means) on Mechanical Turk. HOPs there were 20 quantiles as looping animated strips with no tweening. Findings: adding means "has small biasing effects on both magnitude estimation and decision-making, consistent with discounting uncertainty"; quantile dotplots gave the least bias in magnitude estimation; but "visualization designs which support magnitude estimation are not necessarily best suited as decision aids"; users satisfice with heuristics and often switch strategy. — [Kale, Kay, Hullman 2020, arXiv 2007.14516](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14516v3); [MU Collective project page](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/2020-vis-effect-size-judgements)
- Quantile dotplots were invented explicitly as "a discrete representation of continuous outcomes designed for small screens", for the OneBusAway Seattle transit app. — [Kay, Kola, Hullman, Munson 2016 "When(ish) is My Bus?", CHI (UW IDL page)](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/when-ish-is-my-bus); [MU Collective page](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/when-ish-is-my-bus)
- In an incentivised bus-catching decision experiment, **quantile dotplots with 50 outcomes gave expected payoff 97% of optimal (95% CI [95, 98]), 5 points above a no-uncertainty control, and reduced within-subject SD of decisions by 4 points**; CDF plots performed nearly as well; textual uncertainty performed worse and "was sensitive to the probability interval communicated". — [Fernandes, Walls, Munson, Hullman, Kay 2018, CHI (MU Collective page)](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/uncertainty-bus)
- Error bars: "an error bar can encode any number of values" so readers do not know what it represents; bars produce a "within-the-bar bias" because bars are "large, graphically salient objects that present the visual metaphor of 'containing' values"; the authors propose gradient plots (transparency encodes uncertainty) and violin plots (width) as better for inferential tasks, and report that error bars "make people unjustifiably confident… but other encodings don't". — [Correll & Gleicher 2014, "Error Bars Considered Harmful", TVCG (PDF)](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf); [IEEE VIS 2013 poster](https://ieeevis.org/year/2013/poster/error-bars-considered-harmful)
- Value-Suppressing Uncertainty Palettes (VSUPs): bivariate value+uncertainty encodings interfere with each other; VSUPs give the data a wide slice of the colour channel when uncertainty is low and a narrow one when high, so uncertain values collapse towards a neutral grey. A crowdsourced study found VSUPs made people weigh uncertainty more heavily in decisions than traditional bivariate maps. — [Correll, Moritz, Heer 2018, CHI (Tableau Research)](https://www.tableau.com/research/publications/value-suppressing-uncertainty-palettes)
- Boundary and containment effects: "when boundaries are used in probabilistic data, viewers may misinterpret them as indicating categorical differences"; the containment heuristic (inside vs outside treated as thematically distinct) is "more likely to be triggered when the probability distribution… is visualized with a crisp border rather than a 'fuzzy' one"; "ensemble views and fuzzy borders are likely to mitigate the containment heuristic". — [Padilla, Castro, Hosseinpour 2021 review (working-memory account)](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/a-review-of-uncertainty-visualization-errors-working-memory-as-an-explanatory-theory/812473376734969857-9889); [ICA Proceedings 2021 paper on containment](https://ica-proc.copernicus.org/articles/4/66/2021/ica-proc-4-66-2021.pdf)
- Why uncertainty is usually omitted: a survey of 90 visualisation authors and interviews with 13 influential designers found "a clear contradiction… between authors' acknowledgment of the value of depicting uncertainty and the norm of omitting direct depiction". Reasons were partly UX ("psychologically complex for viewers") and partly rhetorical (authors want to signal confidence). — [Hullman 2020, "Why Authors Don't Visualize Uncertainty", TVCG (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.01697)
- General perceptual guidance: "Extracting global statistics is fast, but comparing between subsets of values is slow"; effective graphics "avoid taxing working memory, guide attention, and respect familiar conventions"; poor design breeds confusion or distrust, especially in viewers with low graphical literacy. — [Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks, Hullman 2021, PSPI](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/15291006211051956/)
- Kay's own caveat on dotplots/HOPs: it is open "how much it matters if users of the visualizations take the draws to be historical data instead of hypothetical observations". — [Kay on Statistical Modeling blog](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/?p=45151)
- Review-level summary: HOPs "tend to help reduce error when laypeople are asked to estimate effect size"; the best display for estimating effect size "may not be the best for decisions". — [Bhatt et al. 2020 review, arXiv 2011.07586](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.07586)

### Inferences
- For Someday's "share of 2,000 futures in which it holds", the evidence favours showing it as a **count of discrete futures** (e.g. 100 or 50 marks, N shaded) rather than as a bare percentage or a smooth gradient. A 10×10 or 5×20 field of marks also reads in under a second as a part-to-whole, which Blascheck's smartwatch work (KQ6) supports.
- For the wealth projection with good/base/poor bands, do **not** draw hard-edged fan bands. Use either (a) a fuzzy/gradient band whose alpha falls off continuously (Correll & Gleicher gradient plot; Padilla fuzzy border), or (b) a thin ensemble of a few dozen faint simulated paths (spaghetti) with the median drawn solid, or (c) an animated HOP that cycles through individual paths at ~400 ms/frame. (a) is the safest default under `prefers-reduced-motion`; (c) is the most honest and the most "alive" while the user holds a control, but must freeze to (a) or (b) when motion is reduced and must never be the only carrier of the information.
- Do not put a thick mean/median line on top of the band and nothing else: Kale 2020 shows means make people discount uncertainty. The median should be present (the owner wants a headline) but visually lighter than the field of outcomes, or drawn as the 50th of the dots rather than as a privileged line.
- VSUP logic transfers to the headline number: the £/month figure can be rendered at full accent saturation when confidence is high and desaturate towards neutral as the share of surviving futures falls, so the number itself carries uncertainty without a second element. That satisfies "nothing said twice".
- Because text probability intervals performed worst in Fernandes 2018, the headline should not lean on phrases like "between £1,900 and £2,400".
- Hullman 2020 implies the owner's instinct to keep uncertainty on the home screen is the unusual, defensible position; the market norm of omitting it is rhetorical, not evidential.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests HOPs or quantile dotplots **on a phone while the user is simultaneously manipulating an input** (the "answer moves while the hand is on the control" case). Frame-rate evidence (400 ms/frame) comes from desktop crowd studies. Treat HOP-while-dragging as a hypothesis to prototype-test (brief §D), not as evidenced.
- The 2020 Kale study abstract does not give per-condition decision-quality numbers for HOPs vs dotplots; I could not open the full PDF to extract them.
- No direct evidence found on how many dots is right for a 390 px-wide phone (Fernandes used 50 on a phone-sized mock; Kay 2016 used 20 and 50); dot count vs legibility at phone scale is untested here.

## KQ2. Fan charts (Bank of England) and the cone of uncertainty (hurricanes): what the research found and how a planner should avoid the failures

### Takeaway
The cone of uncertainty is the cautionary tale: a crisp boundary around a path is read as a growing object and as a categorical inside/outside, and ensembles of individual tracks are misread less (though people over-weight points that happen to lie on a drawn line). The Bank of England's 2026 experimental evidence, by contrast, finds fan charts well understood by UK lay participants and best at jointly conveying expectation and uncertainty, and that the public "consistently underestimates" uncertainty, so a fan that shows it is protective. The reconciliation is in the edges and the layering: soft-edged, graded, many-layered fans with a visible central path work; a single hard cone does not.

### Cited Findings
- Padilla, Ruginski & Creem-Regehr (2017, Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications): the cone of uncertainty "depicts a hurricane's possible path, with no information about size, but study participants were likely to interpret the display as showing a storm growing in size and intensity"; "summary displays were more likely to be misinterpreted than ensemble displays"; but with ensembles "participants were more likely to predict greater damage to an oil rig located on an ensemble line compared to one not falling on a line"; ensembles are "promising for high-level decisions such as regions to evacuate" but not for "communicating impact in specific locations". — [EurekAlert release, University of Utah](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/558221); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/10/171005151140.htm)
- The same group's 2021 review attributes these to boundary/containment heuristics triggered by crisp borders, mitigated by fuzzy borders and ensemble views. — [ICA Proceedings 2021](https://ica-proc.copernicus.org/articles/4/66/2021/ica-proc-4-66-2021.pdf)
- Bank of England Staff Working Paper No. 1,196 (McMahon, Naylor, Rholes, Rickards, 17 July 2026), Part I compared fan charts, dot plots, box-and-whisker plots, speedometers and ranges; Part II compared point forecasts with fan charts across 1,600 UK participants in four stages. "We find that fan charts are well understood and perform best at jointly conveying both expectations and uncertainty." Point forecasts anchor expectations slightly more at first, but "forecast errors significantly de-anchor expectations, particularly for 'unlucky' errors"; fan charts reduce this, acting as an "'insurance policy'". "The public consistently underestimates the degree of uncertainty", and fan charts help people learn more realistic uncertainty perceptions. — [Bank of England working paper page](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty); [PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty.pdf)
- Context: the paper follows the Bernanke Review's recommendation to discontinue the Bank's fan charts; the authors argue that dropping them "could weaken credibility" and that fan-style visualisations "should be retained but paired with clear narrative explanations". — [TSE summary](https://www.tse-fr.eu/anchors-aweigh-effect-communicating-forecast-uncertainty); [CEPR DP21631](https://cepr.org/publications/dp21631)
- Franconeri et al.: viewers extract global shape fast but compare subsets slowly; familiar conventions help. — [Franconeri et al. 2021](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/15291006211051956/)

### Inferences
- A wealth projection on Someday's home should be a **graded fan with soft edges and no single hard outline** (the BoE fan is nine nested bands of graded colour, which is effectively a discretised gradient plot), not a two-line "good/poor" envelope. Two crisp lines around a median is the cone of uncertainty in a different domain and will be read as "my money is a thing that grows and shrinks", not "these are where the paths could be".
- The BoE result that fans beat "speedometers" and "ranges" for joint expectation+uncertainty is a direct warning against a confidence **gauge** (a single needle on an arc) as the primary encoding of the 2,000-futures share. A gauge is a point estimate in costume.
- Because people over-weight whatever lies on a drawn line, the three named bad histories (Japan 1990, 1970s, 2008) should not be drawn as three salient lines inside the fan on the default view; they will be read as the three most likely futures. Show them as a **single floor figure** ("safe £/month under the worst real history") or as a marker at the band's lower edge, with the three paths revealed on demand (turn/flip), not at rest.
- The "unlucky error de-anchors" finding maps onto retirement: a user who saw a confident point estimate and then lives a 2008 will lose trust in the tool; one who saw the fan will not. This is the evidential case for Honest (criterion 5) also being good for Legible-over-time.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Padilla 2017 primary text (Springer redirect loop, core.ac.uk 403); participant numbers and effect sizes above come from the university press release and ScienceDaily. Numbers should be re-checked against DOI 10.1186/s41235-017-0076-1 before being quoted.
- The BoE paper is a working paper (not peer-reviewed) and Part I participant numbers are not on the landing page.
- No research found specifically on **personal-finance** fan charts (e.g. Vanguard/Nutmeg projection fans) as opposed to macro forecasts.

## KQ3. Retirement/financial-projection studies: Monte Carlo "probability of success", framing (success vs failure, % vs N-in-10), UK Pensions Dashboards / MoneyHelper research, FCA/BIT work

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed evidence that bears most directly on Someday's headline is Goldstein, Hershfield & Benartzi: people judge adequacy of a **monthly income** far better than a lump sum, so "£ a month" is the right headline. On "probability of success", the controlled-experiment literature is thin; the strongest material is practitioner analysis (Kitces/Tharp) arguing that "failure" framing is misleading, that clients react to the badness of the tail rather than its probability, and that success rate hides the magnitude of shortfall. UK dashboard research (Ipsos for PDP, BIT) is about engagement and completeness, not about how people read projections; no FCA occasional paper testing drawdown projection formats was found.

### Cited Findings
- "The Illusion of Wealth and Its Reversal" (Goldstein, Hershfield, Benartzi, J. Marketing Research 53(5), 2016): three experiments comparing lump sums with equivalent monthly lifetime income. At the three lowest wealth levels respondents rated the lump sum more adequate than the monthly equivalent; at the three highest the illusion reversed. Mechanism: people "can more easily judge what a monthly amount would buy"; recommendation that providers "focus on monthly income projections more than on lump-sum projections". — [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=595240); [submitted manuscript PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5dd05454f1a7771855d537b7/t/5dd1b04e6d59eb44eaf40443/1574023247476/Goldstein_Hershfield_Benartzi_Submitted_JMR.pdf)
- Distribution Builder (Sharpe, Goldstein, Blythe 2000; Goldstein, Johnson, Sharpe, J. Consumer Research 35(3) 2008): users drag markers ("people") into rows of retirement income expressed as % of pre-retirement income; "each pattern is equivalent to a probability distribution over levels of wealth"; cost shown on a budget meter; a feedback stage removes markers one by one "until only her own remains". Rationale for ~100 markers: "equating the number of people associated with an outcome with the probability of that outcome", citing Gigerenzer on frequencies. The design paper itself flags "serious questions about the ability of a user to fully understand the trade-offs". — [Sharpe, Goldstein, Blythe, "The Distribution Builder" (Stanford HTML)](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/qpaper/qpaper.html); [JCR 2008 record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:v:35:y:2008:i:3:p:440-456)
- Kitces: calling a 10% chance "failure" overstates it; proposes "probability of adjustment" and "probability of excess" instead. — [Kitces, Renaming the outcomes of a Monte Carlo projection](https://www.kitces.com/blog/renaming-the-outcomes-of-a-monte-carlo-retirement-projection/)
- Tharp & Kitces, the "wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy": a client's reaction to 90% success may really reflect how much they dislike the 10% case, and a plan that hits the 10% is judged "wrong" after the fact. — [Kitces blog](https://www.kitces.com/blog/the-wrong-side-of-maybe-fallacy-and-the-interpretation-of-monte-carlo-analysis/); [PortfolioConstruction Forum PDF](https://www.portfolioconstruction.com.au/obj/articles_perspectives/PortfolioConstruction-Forum_Michael-Kitces_Derek-Tharp_The-wrong-side-of-maybe-fallacy.pdf)
- Kitces: a probability of success "says nothing about the magnitude of failure", and small spending adjustments often rescue a "failing" plan; under the usual framing, 50% is the balance point between over- and under-spending. — [Minimum odds article](https://www.kitces.com/blog/monte-carlo-retirement-projection-probability-success-adjustment-minimum-odds/); [Over/under-spend article](https://www.kitces.com/blog/retirement-income-risk-monte-carlo-probability-sucess-over-under-spend/)
- UK Pensions Dashboards Programme qualitative research (Ipsos, 110 in-depth interviews): "strong potential to drive increased pensions knowledge and engagement"; tools letting users adjust contributions, retirement ages and lifestyle options were seen as most valuable; user-friendliness stressed. — [Ipsos PDP qualitative summary report 2022 (PDF)](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2022-01/PDP-qualitative-summary-report-2022.pdf); [MaPS research page](https://maps.org.uk/en/publications/research/2021/pensions-dashboard-user-research)
- Ipsos quantitative: 57% likely to use dashboards; uptake "heavily dependent" on information available at launch. — [Pensions Age](https://www.pensionsage.com/pa/Over-half-of-people-likely-to-use-pensions-dashboards.php); [Pensions Age on launch dependence](https://www.pensionsage.com/pa/Uptake-of-dashboards-heavily-dependent-on-information-available-at-launch.php)
- ABI/BritainThinks: most people want to "actively engage" with pensions on dashboards "rather than use a read-only, static website". — [ABI](https://www.abi.org.uk/news/news-articles/2022/01/interactive-pensions-dashboards-needed-to-boost-pension-engagement/)
- Behavioural Insights Team report for PDP: "conflicting findings" on user tolerance of incomplete dashboards; Danish usage rose sharply once all providers were on; "little published data on what information users actually use on a live dashboard"; urges against "set and forget". — [Pensions Age coverage](https://pensionsage.com/pa/PDP-urged-to-undertake-continued-testing-of-dashboard-following-launch.php)
- FCA Occasional Paper 65 (Sept 2024) is a field trial with 82,000+ customers on **when** to engage pension customers, not on projection formats. — [FCA OP65 PDF](https://fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/op65-testing-engage-pension-customers.pdf)
- FCA 2018 proposals: drawdown Key Features Illustrations to take inflation into account and make charges more prominent (consultation-era). — [Mondaq summary](https://www.mondaq.com/Article/727472)
- Spiegelhalter, Pearson & Short (Science 333:1393, 2011) is the standard reference on words/numbers/visuals for future uncertainty; the companion site's statin icon array shows "100 possible 10-year futures… in 7 possible futures he will have a heart attack or stroke, but in 3 futures [it] will be prevented". — [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Sci...333.1393S); [Understanding Uncertainty "Visualising uncertainty"](https://understandinguncertainty.org/visualising-uncertainty)

### Inferences
- Headline as **£ a month, after tax, today's money** is the evidenced choice (Illusion of Wealth). The pot total should not sit beside it at equal weight.
- The 2,000-futures share should be worded and drawn as **futures that hold vs futures that need an adjustment**, never "success/failure", and should be accompanied by the magnitude of the downside, which Someday already has as "how far it could fall". Showing the floor figure next to the share directly answers the wrong-side-of-maybe problem: the user sees both how often and how bad.
- The Distribution Builder is a 25-year-old precedent for the exact physicalisation Someday wants: **100 people-markers, rows of monthly income**, a budget constraint, and a reveal that removes markers one by one. A field of 100 marks where N are lit, with the user's spending level as a row/threshold, is both a quantile dotplot (KQ1) and a Distribution Builder, and is a non-chart that a stranger reads as "most of them are fine".
- UK dashboard research says users want to **adjust** age, contributions and lifestyle and see the result; it does not say anything about how to show the result, so Someday's home is in uncharted territory relative to PDP/MoneyHelper research.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment found that tests lay interpretation of Monte Carlo "probability of success" under different framings (success/failure, adjustment, % vs "N in 10") in a retirement context. Kitces/Tharp is practitioner reasoning, not measured behaviour.
- No FCA Occasional Paper found on behavioural testing of pension/drawdown projection formats; the FCA Retirement Outcomes Review and Thinks Insight/ABI work on lump-sum guidance were not about visual format.
- Could not open the Goldstein/Johnson/Sharpe 2008 JCR paper (403) to extract what distributions participants actually chose or consistency findings.
- Spiegelhalter 2011 full text was not readable (binary PDF); its principle list is not quoted here.
- No research found on how UK users read **inflation-adjusted ("today's money")** monthly figures vs nominal.

## KQ4. Direct manipulation and fluid interaction: Victor, Case, NYT, Distill — design rules for live input→output coupling and for showing a whole parameter space (the "edge" map)

### Takeaway
Victor's rules are: the interactivity serves an argument, not a sandbox; adjustable values live inside the thing being read and are changed by drag, with every dependent representation updating together; and the whole-parameter-space view ("show all parameters") is only useful if the user can step back down to a single concrete case, because "insight comes not at any one level of abstraction, but in the transitions between them". Distill's survey of interactive articles adds the research: aesthetics drive engagement, prediction-before-reveal improves recall (Kim et al. 2017), details-on-demand reduces load, and interactivity that is not central to the message goes unused.

### Cited Findings
- "The interactivity itself is not really the point"; a reactive document is "like a spreadsheet without the spreadsheet" and "a spreadsheet is not an explanation". Adjustable values are marked in the text and changed by click-and-drag; in the filter example "six representations respond together". "Most interactive widgets dump the user in a sandbox and say 'figure it out for yourself'. Those are not explanations." 2024 postscript: he meant a written argument "backed by explorable computational models" whose assumptions are visible and editable. — [Bret Victor, Explorable Explanations (2011)](http://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/)
- Ladder of Abstraction: (1) control time with a slider; (2) show all time (the trajectory replaces the moving object, "we have abstracted over time"); (3) control the parameter; (4) show all parameters (overlay trajectories or plot a metric against the parameter). "A designer needs direct, interactive control over the independent variables of the system." Of the full parameter-space chart: "this representation bears no resemblance to the motion of a car", lower-level detail is available only "by interactively stepping down"; each earlier view is "a slice" of the space; insight is "not at any one level of abstraction, but in the transitions between them". — [Bret Victor, Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction (2011)](http://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/)
- Nicky Case defines an explorable as something that "1) teaches something, and 2) is more interactive than a boring ol' quiz with only one right answer"; the roots are journalism and textbooks; the form is "interactive non-fiction". — [Nicky Case blog, Explorable Explanations](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations); [Data Stories ep. 103](https://datastori.es/103-explorable-explanations-with-nicky-case/)
- Distill, "Communicating with Interactive Articles" (Hohman et al. 2020), five affordances with evidence: aesthetics → positive attitude and engagement; animation helps "state transitions, uncertainty, causality, and narrative" though debated vs well-designed statics; multiple complementary representations beat redundant ones (Mayer's multimedia and redundancy principles); self-explanation and the testing effect improve learning; "learners perform better when information is segmented"; details-on-demand (Shneiderman's "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand") reduces load. Prediction-before-reveal ("You Draw It", NYT 2015): Kim et al. 2017 found "using visualizations as a prompt is an effective way to encourage readers to engage in self explanation" and improve recall; showing others' predictions did not necessarily help recall. Warnings: "In the worst case, interactivity may be distracting to readers or the functionality may go unused"; the NYT "only a fraction interact" rule of thumb may push designers to a suboptimal format; "other research found many readers, including on mobile, want interactivity when it is central to the message". Accessibility of dynamic content is listed as an open challenge. — [Distill 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)
- Direct-manipulation canon referenced by Distill: Shneiderman's visual-information-seeking mantra. — [Distill 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)
- Goldstein & Sharpe's Distribution Builder is itself a direct-manipulation explorable with a hard budget constraint ("cannot select a final pattern that does not use 100 units of the budget"), i.e. the control space is constrained by the model, not free. — [Sharpe et al.](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/qpaper/qpaper.html)

### Inferences
- Rule for Someday's live coupling: **the control is the number**. Victor's draggable in-text values mean the "£2,100 a month" and "stop at 62" figures should themselves be the grabbable things (drag the numeral, scrub the age), rather than sliders beneath labels. Every dependent view (fan, count of futures, floor, years-it-lasts) updates in the same frame. That is also the strongest answer to "One object" and "Nothing said twice".
- The brief's "edge" map (stop-age × spending, which pairs hold) is Victor's level-3 "show all parameters" plane. His rule means it must not be the resting state of the home: the home is a concrete slice (this age, this spending, this fan), and the edge is reached by **zooming out** from the current point, with the current point marked, and tapping any cell steps back down to a concrete plan. A gesture of pinch-out from the dial to the plane and pinch-in to return is the literal "ladder".
- Prediction-before-reveal is a cheap, evidenced onboarding device: ask the user to set "what do you think you'll get a month?" before the engine answers; recall of the real answer improves. It also fits the owner's desire for a moment when the plan "completes".
- Case's and Victor's warnings against sandboxes mean the home should carry a one-line authored reading of the result ("holds in 86 of 100 futures; falls to £1,700 after a 2008 start"), not just the instrument.
- Distill's "interactivity goes unused unless central" and "segment information" supports putting the six inputs on one instrument where every face changes the answer, and against six separate controls of equal weight.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on **drag-the-numeral** (Victor-style scrubbable numbers) vs sliders for accuracy or speed on touch screens.
- NYT "You Draw It" engagement metrics are unpublished (Distill notes publishers rarely share metrics).
- No evidence found on the comprehension of 2D parameter-space heatmaps (age × spending) by lay audiences on phones specifically.

## KQ5. Data physicalisation and data humanism (Lupi, Bremer, Wu, Stefaner, Accurat, Fathom, Fragapane, Posavec): visual grammars that could carry a lifetime of years and three market paths on a phone without a conventional chart

### Takeaway
Lupi's manifesto licenses exactly what the brief asks for: unconventional, dense, imperfect-looking encodings that "promote slowness", provided they still answer a question; but her own principle is that complexity must open eyes to fresh knowledge, not decorate. The transferable grammars are: a unit field of individual marks (one per simulated future), strands or threads that fan from a single point (one per path), and radial rings of years, each with a precedent in this school. The evidence from KQ1/KQ6 says the unit field is the one of these that lay people also read accurately.

### Cited Findings
- Lupi's Data Humanism manifesto (2017) has four headed sections: Embrace complexity ("Whenever the main purpose of data visualization is to open people's eyes to fresh knowledge, it is impractical to avoid a certain level of visual complexity"; "Dense and unconventional data visualizations promote slowness"); Move beyond standards ("One size does not fit all"; "Sketching with data… leads to designs that are uniquely customized for the specific type of data problems"); Sneak context in (always); Remember data is imperfect ("We should experiment with how to visualize uncertainty, possible errors and imperfections in our data"; "If we can create visuals that encourage careful reading and personal engagement, people will find more and more real value in data"). — [Giorgia Lupi, Data Humanism manifesto](https://giorgialupi.com/data-humanism-my-manifesto-for-a-new-data-wold)
- A 2025 characterisation paper condenses the manifesto's 13 principle-pairs into four overarching principles — "Embrace complexity, Move beyond standards, Sneak context in, and Remember that data is imperfect" — and notes they "remain loosely defined in research contexts". — [Data Humanism Decoded, arXiv 2509.00440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00440v1)
- Lupi's own philosophy page frames the approach as reconnecting data "to what it stands for: our lives, stories and ideas"; Dear Data (with Posavec) was a year of hand-drawn postcards of personal data. — [giorgialupi.com/philosophy](https://giorgialupi.com/philosophy)
- Bremer's radial practice: at WebExpo 2025 she live-coded a 24-slice time ring with curved hour labels and "a circular line showing the average births per minute", noting zero was not a meaningful baseline for that data; "The Baby Spike" (with Zan Armstrong, Scientific American 2017) is the canonical radial time piece. In 2017 she said "usually what I have in mind isn't quite the default setting… and that forces me to try and be creative". — [WebExpo blog on Bremer's radial story](https://webexpo.net/blog/from-a-blank-screen-to-birth-rhythms-nadieh-bremer-live-codes-a-radial-story/); [beyond tellerrand 2017 speaker page](https://beyondtellerrand.com/events/dusseldorf-2017/speakers/nadieh-bremer)
- Unit visualisations: Distill reports that person-shaped glyphs did not add empathic response (Boy et al. 2017) but photographs did increase interest and recall; unit visualisations "can make data feel like real people". — [Distill 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)
- The Distribution Builder is a unit visualisation of futures with a frequency rationale (each marker = one possible you). — [Sharpe et al.](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/qpaper/qpaper.html)
- Controlled evidence against the radial-time instinct for untrained users: "The assumed intuitiveness of visualizing daily patterns in an analog clock layout could not be shown"; "visualizations for the masses should rely on linear bar charts instead of radial rose charts"; but radial charts are "often seen as more aesthetic and memorable" and "more fun". — [Waldner et al. 2019/2020 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1907.13534)
- Tim Urban's "Your Life in Weeks" (Wait But Why, 2014): a 52 × 90 grid, one box per week, past boxes filled and future boxes empty; widely cloned as posters and tools. — [Wait But Why original (not fetched; cited via)](https://weekplan.net/mini-tools/life-in-weeks/); [Engineered.at write-up](https://engineered.at/articles/terrifyingly-inspiring-my-life-in-weeks)

### Inferences
- The grammar that satisfies both Lupi and the perception literature is a **field of units**: 100 marks for the 100 (of 2,000, scaled) futures, lit where the plan holds. It is non-chart, dense, "promotes slowness" on inspection yet reads as a proportion in a glance, and it is the Distribution Builder's "you are one of these people" made visible. Marks should be abstract (dots, dashes, grains), not person icons: Boy et al. found anthropomorphic glyphs add nothing.
- A second honest grammar is **strands**: 20–50 faint simulated wealth paths drawn as threads from the stop age, thickening where they bunch; the three named histories are three strands that can be lit on demand. Strands are an ensemble (KQ2), which is misread less than a cone, and they look like data art rather than a chart. Risk: people over-read any strand that is highlighted (Padilla's "on-the-line" bias), so at rest no strand should be highlighted except the median.
- A **ring of years** (stop age to plan-to age around the dial) is attractive for the instrument metaphor but the evidence says locating/reading a value on a ring is slower and less accurate (KQ6). Acceptable use: the ring is the *control* for age and horizon (turning it is natural) while the *data* (bands, strands, units) is read linearly or as a count. Do not make the ring the primary carrier of the wealth projection.
- "Life in Weeks" is the mass-culture proof that a grid of filled/unfilled units of time is instantly legible and emotionally charged; a Someday variant (one mark per year from now to the plan-to age, filled where the plan holds in the base case, dimming towards the poor case) would carry "how long it lasts" without a chart. It is also radial-free and reduced-motion-safe.
- Imperfection as an aesthetic (Lupi, Dear Data) justifies soft edges, grain and slight irregularity in the units and strands. This is also what Correll/Padilla ask for functionally (fuzzy borders). Form and evidence align here, which is rare.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation found of Lupi/Accurat/Fathom/Stefaner pieces for reading accuracy; this school is assessed critically (Waldner) but not studied as such.
- Could not find a primary statement by Bremer on why she chooses radial; the rationale above is inferred from her practice.
- Shirley Wu, Moritz Stefaner (e.g. OECD Better Life Index flower glyphs), Fathom, Fragapane and Posavec were not examined individually within the tool budget; no claims are made about specific works beyond Dear Data.

## KQ6. Radial time encodings (spirals, polar area, radial bar, "life in weeks" grids): reading accuracy of radial vs linear time, and when radial is acceptable

### Takeaway
Across three independent studies (Waldner on daily patterns, Brehmer on ranges over time on phones, Blascheck on smartwatches) radial layouts were slower, often less accurate, and universally less preferred for locating and reading values; the one exception is part-to-whole single values (donut/ring progress), where a ring is the fastest glanceable form (~220–300 ms). Radial is acceptable when the thing being shown is one proportion, when the cycle is genuinely meaningful, or when aesthetics/memorability outweigh efficiency; it is not acceptable as the primary carrier of a time series the user has to read.

### Cited Findings
- Waldner et al. (TVCG 2019/2020), 92 non-expert crowd workers, radial vs linear within-subjects, 12 h vs 24 h between-subjects, traffic accidents by hour. Locating an hour: ~7.4 s radial vs ~5.2 s linear; error 30% (12 h radial) vs 9% (12 h linear). Reading a value: error 41% (12 h radial) vs 14% (24 h linear). Locating the maximum: 24 h linear fastest (~3.4 s). Ratings: linear ~1.2 points higher on a 5-point scale (24 h linear 4.3; 12 h radial 2.95). "Visualizations for the masses should rely on linear bar charts instead of radial rose charts." Radial is "often seen as more aesthetic and memorable". — [Waldner et al. (ar5iv HTML)](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1907.13534); [TU Wien publication page](https://cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2019/waldner-2019-rld)
- Brehmer et al., "Visualizing Ranges Over Time on Mobile Phones" (IEEE VIS 2018), 100 MTurk participants (87 analysed), 84 trials each on their own phones, linear vs radial at week/month/year granularity, temperature and sleep ranges. "People are, in general, slower with Radial layouts", especially reading values on an indicated day; comparison tasks about equal; radial reduced accuracy for sleep data on reading values and locating extremes; "participants universally preferred linear layouts, and they felt more confident using linear layouts". Decision guide: "Locating values (quickly)? → Linear"; "Comparing values (unconcerned with efficiency)? → Radial or Linear". "Our experiment was conducted exclusively on mobile phones and we can only speak to that form factor." — [Brehmer, talk and paper page](https://mattbrehmer.ca/talks/ranges/)
- Blascheck et al., "Glanceable Visualization" (InfoVis 2018): bar vs donut vs radial bar on smartwatches, 7/12/24 values, which-is-larger task. Average thresholds under 300 ms (bar), under 220 ms (donut), under 1,780 ms (radial bar). "Bar and donut charts should be preferred on smartwatch displays when quick data comparisons are necessary." Radial bar charts suggested as better "for representing single data points in part-to-whole comparisons, particularly for goal completion". — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/08/GlanceableVis-InfoVis2018.pdf); [project page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=500399)
- Replication with adults 65+: donut 312 ms, bar 485 ms, radial 2,211 ms; at 24 values donut 401 ms vs bar 771 ms. — [While, Blascheck, Gong 2024, arXiv 2403.12343](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.12343)
- A 2023 smartwatch study found bar and radial bar part-to-whole glyphs both beat text, and an analogue clock dial drawn around them did not hurt performance. — [HAL record](https://hal-hprints.archives-ouvertes.fr/LISN-ILDA/hal-04018448v1)
- Earlier eye-tracking (Goldberg & Helfman, cited by Waldner) found reading values on radial charts clearly harder; Fuchs et al. is the counter-example where radial glyphs helped for reading values at specific time points. — [Waldner et al. related work](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1907.13534)
- "Your Life in Weeks" as a linear grid of units (52 × 90). — [Engineered.at](https://engineered.at/articles/terrifyingly-inspiring-my-life-in-weeks)

### Inferences
- The owner's rotating pie/dial is defensible **only as a control and as a single proportion**. A ring whose filled arc is the share of futures that hold (donut) is the fastest-read form in the literature and is radial. A ring whose 60 segments are years with wealth encoded on each is the slowest-read form in the literature.
- Therefore: let the instrument be radial (turn to set age, spending, horizon; the arc shows the proportion), and let the **projection** be a linear strip or a unit grid beneath or inside it. A 390 px screen has room for a ~300 px dial and a ~100 px linear strip.
- The 2023 finding that a clock-dial surround did not impair reading the glyph inside it supports a dial frame around a linear/unit data core.
- Twelve-hour (two-ring) radials were the worst condition in Waldner; by analogy, two concentric rings for two partners' figures is likely to be misread. A couple's second set should be reached by flipping/turning the whole instrument, not by a second ring.
- For older users (Someday's core audience is 45–65), the donut/ring advantage holds or grows (While 2024), which is a point in favour of a ring for the headline proportion specifically.

### Gaps
- No study found of **spirals** (spiral calendars) or polar-area/Nightingale charts for lifetime-scale data with lay readers; the evidence above is for daily/weekly/annual cycles.
- No study found of reading accuracy on a **3D** drum or cylinder (text or data on a rotating surface) versus flat.
- No evidence on radial vs linear for the specific task "how many years until the money runs out".

## KQ7. Cognitive load: simultaneous vs sequential input of ~10 parameters on a phone; dial/knob vs slider vs numeric entry error rates

### Takeaway
The strongest UK evidence on sequencing is GDS's "one thing per page": one question at a time was easier to communicate and complete across many usability tests, which is a warning that six inputs exposed at once on one screen will cost comprehension unless the design focuses attention on one at a time (a drum face, a dial position). On input type, the evidence is thin and mixed: sliders were the most accurate of six tangible self-report inputs (TEI 2022); touchscreen slider precision depends significantly on orientation and visual style (CHI 2019); one mobile study found a rotary "disc" significantly better than a point-slider for numeric entry; and physical dials outperform touchscreen dials. I found no study that gives dial-vs-slider error rates on a phone for finance-scale values.

### Cited Findings
- GDS researched "one thing per page" "in great depth" on Register to vote and GOV.UK Verify; comparing one question per page to all on one page the team "saw many benefits"; "focusing on one thing per page makes that thing much easier to communicate"; GOV.UK Forms testers said one question per page "made the form easier to complete". It is a starting point: group questions when research says so. — [GDS Design Notes, One thing per page (Tim Paul, 2015)](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2015/07/03/one-thing-per-page/); [MoJ pattern library on question pages](https://design-patterns.service.justice.gov.uk/archive/question-pages)
- Adam Silver (practitioner, anecdotal): in hundreds of usability tests over ~10 years, one thing per page "failed only once". — [Smashing Magazine case study](https://smashingmagazine.com/2017/05/better-form-design-one-thing-per-page)
- Distill/Mayer: "learners perform better when information is segmented"; details-on-demand reduces load. — [Distill 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)
- Franconeri et al.: effective graphics "avoid taxing working memory, guide attention". — [Franconeri et al. 2021](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/15291006211051956/)
- Touchscreen sliders (Colley, Mayer, Henze, CHI 2019): "significant effects of slider orientation and style on the precision of the entered values" and systematic offsets depending on style and target value. — [LMU PDF](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/colley2019investigatingthe/colley2019investigatingthe.pdf)
- Tangible self-report devices (TEI 2022, N = 20): of slider, slider with resistance, capacitive touch slider, squeeze, rotary knob and joystick, "participant accuracy was highest using a slider, and lowest using a squeeze-based input"; knob rank not stated in the abstract. — [Aalborg record](https://vbn.aau.dk/en/publications/tangible-self-report-devices-accuracy-and-resolution-of-participa)
- Dials vs sliders on horizontal touchscreens (physical vs virtual): "speed and accuracy differences were seen primarily for dial controls; there was little difference between input conditions for sliders", confirming "the value of physical input devices for parameter control tasks". — [ACM DOI 10.1145/2512349.2512812 via Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2512349.2512812)
- Mobile numeric entry: a rotary "disc concept" vs a point-slider — "a significant superiority of the disc concept" in a user study. — [ACM DOI 10.1145/1409240.1409276](https://www.springerprofessional.de/doi/10.1145/1409240.1409276)
- Circular touchscreens: a model for error rate of rim buttons given angular and radial widths exists (cited in a reference list only). — [Peeref listing](https://peeref.com/works/41020978)
- Victor: a designer "needs direct, interactive control over the independent variables"; readers nudge values by drag with "low barrier". — [Ladder of Abstraction](http://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/)
- Fitts-style and radial-menu literature was not reached within budget (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- "Six inputs feel like one object" and "one thing per page" can be reconciled: the instrument shows all six positions but **focuses one at a time** (the face that is up, the segment under the thumb), with the other five present as small, legible values. That is sequential attention on a simultaneous object, which is what a drum or a crown-and-dial watch does.
- Given sliders' known orientation/style offsets and dials' touchscreen disadvantage, the safest live control for a phone is **a large circular drag whose value is read from a big numeral, not from the angle** (the angle is feedback, the numeral is the value), with detents at round numbers (£50/month, 1 year) and a tap-to-type fallback for exact entry. This matches the mobile disc result and sidesteps the "read the angle" problem.
- Numeric exactness matters more for pot sizes and DB income (large, specific numbers) than for stop age or monthly spend; the design should allow coarse rotary setting for the latter and typed entry for the former without leaving the screen (a tap on the numeral opens the keypad in place).
- The evidence does not support a 3D drum rolled to pick digits (the rejected cryptex): it combines radial reading with multi-step digit entry.

### Gaps
- No study found giving dial vs slider vs keypad **error rates on a phone touchscreen** for money-scale values; the dial/slider papers found are either tangible/physical or horizontal tabletop.
- No Fitts's-law literature on circular drag on phones was examined within budget.
- GDS evidence is on form pages, not instruments; whether its finding transfers to a single-object UI with focus-one-at-a-time is an inference.
- No published study found on simultaneous vs sequential input of ~10 parameters in a calculator-type tool specifically.

## Cross-cutting synthesis for the home (what the evidence collectively supports)

### Takeaway
An honest, legible, one-screen home can be built from three evidenced elements: a **headline monthly figure** that is itself the draggable control and whose saturation encodes confidence (Victor + VSUP + Illusion of Wealth); a **field of discrete futures** (quantile dotplot / Distribution Builder / Life-in-Weeks grammar) carrying the 2,000-futures share and the floor under real bad histories; and a **soft-edged, many-layered fan or faint strand ensemble** for the wealth path, with no crisp outline and with the median visually subordinate. Radial geometry is reserved for the control ring and for the single confidence proportion; time series are read linearly.

### Cited Findings
- Each element above is sourced in KQ1–KQ7; see in particular [Fernandes 2018](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/uncertainty-bus), [Correll & Gleicher 2014](https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Error%20Bars%20Considered%20Harmful%20Exploring%20Alternate%20Encodings%20for%20Mean%20and%20Error.pdf), [Correll, Moritz, Heer 2018](https://www.tableau.com/research/publications/value-suppressing-uncertainty-palettes), [BoE SWP 1196](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty), [Goldstein, Hershfield, Benartzi 2016](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=595240), [Sharpe, Goldstein, Blythe](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/qpaper/qpaper.html), [Victor 2011](http://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/), [Blascheck 2018](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2018/08/GlanceableVis-InfoVis2018.pdf), [Brehmer 2018](https://mattbrehmer.ca/talks/ranges/), [Waldner 2019](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1907.13534), [Lupi 2017](https://giorgialupi.com/data-humanism-my-manifesto-for-a-new-data-wold).

### Inferences
- Scoring against brief §6: this grammar scores high on Honest (fan + units + floor on the home), Live (controls are the numerals), Minimal (confidence lives in the colour of the number and the lit count; nothing repeated), Legible (donut/unit proportion reads in <300 ms; monthly figure is the one people judge well). Its risk is Different: a soft fan and a dot field are known forms; the difference must come from the instrument (ring control, strands, atmosphere), not from inventing new encodings for the uncertainty itself, where the evidence says invention is misread.
- Under `prefers-reduced-motion`: HOP cycling of strands stops; the unit field and gradient fan remain, so no information is lost (HOPs are an enhancement, never the sole carrier).
- Things the evidence warns against on this home: a confidence gauge/speedometer; two crisp band edges; three highlighted history lines at rest; a bold mean line; text intervals; percentages without a count; a ring of years as the data carrier; two concentric rings for a couple; a digit drum.

### Gaps
- The combination has not been tested as a whole anywhere I could find; the brief's §D hallway test should check (i) whether strangers read the lit-unit field as "chance it holds" within ten seconds, (ii) whether the desaturating headline is noticed, (iii) whether a dial-set value is read from the numeral or the angle, and (iv) whether cycling strands while dragging helps or distracts.
